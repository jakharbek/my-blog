SiLU / Swish: an input that gates itself

The activation of modern language models: the input is multiplied by the sigmoid of itself. We take it apart bone by bone: why it has a minimum of −0.278 and a derivative above one, how it relates to ReLU and GELU, where the Swish-β family comes from, and how SiLU works inside the SwiGLU block of LLaMA and Qwen.

$$\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=x\cdot\sigma(x)=\frac{x}{1+e^{-x}}$$

Why we need it

ReLU has two drawbacks: it has a kink at zero, and on the left it throws away the whole signal together with the gradient. SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) works differently: the input $x$ is multiplied by the sigmoid of itself. A large positive input passes almost in full, a large negative one is almost completely suppressed, and near zero the input passes “partially” — smoothly, without kinks.

The same function with a parameter, $x\,\sigma(\beta x)$, is called Swish. With $\beta=1$ it is exactly SiLU; PyTorch and other libraries call it just that: nn.SiLU.

Where it appears:

  • large language models — LLaMA, Qwen, Mistral and PaLM use SiLU inside the SwiGLU block (section 7); in model configs it is the line hidden_act = "silu";
  • computer vision — EfficientNet (2019) is built on Swish, and the YOLO family of detectors uses SiLU starting with YOLOv5;
  • mobile networks — MobileNetV3 uses the “hard” Swish, a cheap piecewise-polynomial approximation $x\cdot\operatorname{ReLU6}(x+3)/6$;
  • reinforcement learning — Elfwing, Uchibe and Doya (2017) used it to approximate value functions under the name SiLU.
The essentials in a minute

$\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=x\,\sigma(x)$. Far to the right it is almost $x$, far to the left almost 0, like ReLU, but smooth. It is non-monotonic: the minimum is $\approx-0.278$ at $x\approx-1.278$. The derivative $\sigma(x)\bigl(1+x(1-\sigma(x))\bigr)$ equals $0.5$ at zero and exceeds 1 for $x>1.278$ (maximum $\approx1.100$). In LLMs SiLU acts as the gate in SwiGLU: $\operatorname{SiLU}(W_1x)\odot W_3x$.

Intuition: the input decides how much of itself to let through

Picture a tap whose handle is turned by the flow itself. The sigmoid $\sigma(x)$ is how far the tap is open, from 0 to 1. If the input is large and positive, the tap is almost fully open ($\sigma\approx1$) and the output almost equals the input. If the input is strongly negative, the tap is almost closed and the output is close to zero. This mechanism is called self-gating: the input is both the signal and the decision about how much of the signal to pass.

Drag the blue point. The teal curve is the gate $\sigma(x)$, the blue one is the product $x\cdot\sigma(x)$. Look at the area around $x=-1$: the gate is already partly closed, but not fully, while the input is negative — and the output dips below zero.

What to notice

On the right the blue curve hugs the line $y=x$, on the left it hugs zero: in those areas SiLU hardly differs from ReLU. The whole difference sits in the band of roughly $-5<x<5$, where the gate is partly open. That is where ReLU makes a sharp kink, while SiLU makes a smooth bend with a small “dip” on the left.

The formula, bone by bone

Let us build SiLU from two familiar parts and look at it in several forms.

$$\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=\underbrace{x}_{\text{signal}}\cdot\underbrace{\sigma(x)}_{\text{gate}}=\frac{x}{1+e^{-x}}$$
$x$
the neuron’s input (pre-activation); it is also the signal to be let through;
$\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$
the sigmoid, a number in $(0,\,1)$ — the share of the signal that will pass;
$x\cdot\sigma(x)$
the result: with $\sigma\approx1$ the output is $\approx x$, with $\sigma\approx0$ it is $\approx0$.

Through the hyperbolic tangent ($\sigma(x)=\tfrac12(1+\tanh\tfrac x2)$) we get a form that explains a lot:

$$\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=\frac{x}{2}\Bigl(1+\tanh\frac{x}{2}\Bigr)=\underbrace{\frac{x}{2}}_{\text{odd part}}+\underbrace{\frac{x}{2}\tanh\frac{x}{2}}_{\text{even part}}$$

Compare with ReLU: $\operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{|x|}{2}$. Their odd parts are identical, and the even part of SiLU, $\frac x2\tanh\frac x2$, is a smoothed absolute value $\frac{|x|}{2}$: for large $|x|$ the tangent is $\pm1$ and they coincide, while near zero the corner is replaced by the parabola $\approx\frac{x^2}{4}$.

Properties

  • Smoothness: SiLU is infinitely differentiable — there is not a single kink.
  • Asymptotes: $\operatorname{SiLU}(x)-x\to0$ as $x\to+\infty$ and $\operatorname{SiLU}(x)\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$, exponentially fast in both cases.
  • Non-monotonicity: the function decreases on $(-\infty,\,x^*)$ and increases on $(x^*,\,+\infty)$, where $x^*\approx-1.2785$.
  • Bounded below: the range is $[-0.2785,\,+\infty)$. It is unbounded above.
  • Link with ReLU: $\operatorname{ReLU}(x)-\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=|x|\,\sigma(-|x|)$ — an even function between 0 and $0.2785$. SiLU always lies below ReLU, but never more than $0.2785$ below.
  • Antisymmetry of the difference: $\operatorname{SiLU}(x)-\operatorname{SiLU}(-x)=x$, because $\sigma(x)+\sigma(-x)=1$.
  • Convexity: the function is convex on $(-2.3994,\,2.3994)$ and concave outside it; these are the inflection points.

The minimum: where and why −0.278

The minimum is where the derivative is zero. The result is neatly written through the Lambert W function — the solution of $We^{W}=c$:

$$x^*=-1-W\!\left(e^{-1}\right)\approx-1.2785,\qquad \operatorname{SiLU}(x^*)=x^*+1=-W\!\left(e^{-1}\right)\approx-0.2785$$
Derivation through the Lambert W function

The derivative (section 5) is $\sigma(x)\bigl(1+x(1-\sigma(x))\bigr)$. The sigmoid is positive, so only the bracket can vanish: $1+x(1-\sigma)=0$, i.e. $1-\sigma(x)=-1/x$.

This immediately gives the value of the minimum: $\sigma(x^*)=1+1/x^*$, and $\operatorname{SiLU}(x^*)=x^*\sigma(x^*)=x^*+1$.

Now find $x^*$. Since $1-\sigma(x)=\sigma(-x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{x}}$, the equation becomes $1+e^{x}=-x$. Put $u=-x-1$; then $x=-u-1$ and

$$1+e^{-u-1}=u+1\;\Longleftrightarrow\;e^{-u}e^{-1}=u\;\Longleftrightarrow\;u\,e^{u}=e^{-1}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;u=W(e^{-1})\approx0.2785$$

Hence $x^*=-1-W(e^{-1})\approx-1.2785$ and $\operatorname{SiLU}(x^*)=x^*+1=-W(e^{-1})\approx-0.2785$. A pretty coincidence: the depth of the dip and its distance from the point $x=-1$ are the same number.

The Swish-β family

Adding a steepness $\beta$ to the gate gives the Swish family:

$$\operatorname{Swish}_\beta(x)=x\,\sigma(\beta x),\qquad \lim_{\beta\to\infty}\operatorname{Swish}_\beta(x)=\operatorname{ReLU}(x),\qquad \operatorname{Swish}_0(x)=\frac{x}{2}$$

With $\beta=0$ the gate is always half open, and the function is linear. As $\beta\to\infty$ the sigmoid turns into a step, and we get ReLU. Swish-β is a bridge between a linear function and ReLU, and SiLU is the point $\beta=1$ on that bridge. The substitution $u=\beta x$ shows that all members of the family are one and the same graph at a different scale: $\operatorname{Swish}_\beta(x)=\frac{1}{\beta}\operatorname{SiLU}(\beta x)$.

Ramachandran, Zoph and Le (2017) found $x\,\sigma(\beta x)$ by an automated search among thousands of candidate formulas and called it Swish; a learnable $\beta$ added almost nothing, and in practice $\beta=1$ stuck.

The derivative: above one and below zero

By the product rule the derivative of SiLU is made of two parts: the gate and how fast the gate opens.

$$\operatorname{SiLU}'(x)=\underbrace{\sigma(x)}_{\text{gate}}+\underbrace{x\,\sigma'(x)}_{\text{gate opening}}=\sigma(x)\bigl(1+x\,(1-\sigma(x))\bigr)$$
Step-by-step derivation

The product rule: $(x\cdot\sigma(x))'=1\cdot\sigma(x)+x\cdot\sigma'(x)$.

The derivative of the sigmoid is known: $\sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))$ (derived on the sigmoid page). Substitute and factor out $\sigma(x)$:

$$\operatorname{SiLU}'(x)=\sigma(x)+x\,\sigma(x)\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr)=\sigma(x)\bigl(1+x(1-\sigma(x))\bigr)$$

One more useful form uses the value of the function itself, $f=\operatorname{SiLU}(x)$: expanding the brackets gives $\sigma+x\sigma-x\sigma^2=f+\sigma(1-f)$. Implementations use it: the backward pass needs only the already computed $f$ and $\sigma$.

$$\operatorname{SiLU}'(x)=\operatorname{SiLU}(x)+\sigma(x)\bigl(1-\operatorname{SiLU}(x)\bigr)$$

Properties of the derivative:

  • $\operatorname{SiLU}'(0)=\sigma(0)=\tfrac12$ — at zero the slope is exactly one half;
  • $\operatorname{SiLU}'(x)+\operatorname{SiLU}'(-x)=1$ — the derivative of the identity $\operatorname{SiLU}(x)-\operatorname{SiLU}(-x)=x$;
  • the derivative is negative for $x<-1.2785$ (left of the minimum), with the smallest value $\approx-0.0998$ at $x\approx-2.3994$;
  • the derivative is above 1 for $x>1.2785$ — by the symmetry of the previous item — with a maximum of $\approx1.0998$ at $x\approx2.3994$; then it tends to 1 from above.

The points $\pm2.3994$ are the roots of $x\tanh\frac{x}{2}=2$: that is where the second derivative vanishes

$$\operatorname{SiLU}''(x)=\sigma(x)\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr)\Bigl(2-x\tanh\frac{x}{2}\Bigr),\qquad \operatorname{SiLU}''(0)=\frac12$$
Why a derivative above 1 is fine

A slope above 1 means that for $x>1.28$ SiLU is “catching up” with the line $y=x$, from which it fell behind near zero. For $x>0$ the output of SiLU never exceeds the input ($\sigma<1$), so nothing explodes: the slope is only slightly above 1 and quickly returns to 1.

Smoothness and relatives: ReLU and GELU

Three functions on one plot: ReLU, SiLU and GELU $=x\,\Phi(x)$, where $\Phi$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal. Look not only at the functions but also at the first and second derivatives: that is where the difference in smoothness shows.

  • ReLU: the first derivative is a step, the second a delta function. For the optimizer the loss surface is made of flat pieces with sharp edges.
  • SiLU and GELU: the derivatives are continuous, the second derivatives are smooth “humps” peaking at zero ($0.5$ for SiLU, $2\varphi(0)\approx0.798$ for GELU). The loss landscape becomes smooth, and the gradient changes gradually.
  • GELU ≈ Swish with $\beta=1.702$: $x\,\Phi(x)\approx x\,\sigma(1.702x)$ with a largest error of about $0.02$. GELU is a slightly “steeper” SiLU: its dip is shallower ($-0.170$ versus $-0.278$), and its maximum derivative is higher ($1.129$ versus $1.100$).

The probabilistic meaning: a random gate

SiLU and GELU share an explanation. Let a neuron pass the input in full if the input is larger than a random noise $\varepsilon$, and block it completely otherwise: $y=x\cdot[\varepsilon\le x]$. Then the mean output is

$$\mathbb E[y]=x\cdot P(\varepsilon\le x)=x\cdot F_\varepsilon(x)=\begin{cases}x\,\sigma(x)=\operatorname{SiLU}(x), & \varepsilon\sim\text{Logistic}(0,1),\\ x\,\Phi(x)=\operatorname{GELU}(x), & \varepsilon\sim N(0,1).\end{cases}$$

The sigmoid is the distribution function of the logistic law, $\Phi$ that of the normal one. So SiLU is an “averaged ReLU with a random threshold”: instead of cutting exactly at zero, the neuron cuts at a random point near zero, and on average the result is a smooth curve.

SwiGLU: SiLU in large language models

In a transformer the attention block is followed by a fully connected block (feed-forward network, FFN). The classic version is $W_2\,\operatorname{ReLU}(W_1x)$ (or GELU instead of ReLU). Shazeer (2020) proposed replacing it with a gated block (gated linear unit), where one branch decides how much of the other to let through:

$$\operatorname{FFN}_{\text{SwiGLU}}(x)=W_2\bigl(\operatorname{SiLU}(W_1x)\odot W_3x\bigr)$$
$x\in\mathbb R^{d}$
the token vector of dimension $d$ (for LLaMA-7B $d=4096$);
$W_1x$
the gate branch: through SiLU it decides which components to pass and how much;
$W_3x$
the value branch: linear, with no activation — what gets passed;
$\odot$
the element-wise product of two vectors of dimension $h$ (the hidden size);
$W_2$
the projection back to dimension $d$.

For a single component this is a function of two numbers: $g(a,b)=\operatorname{SiLU}(a)\cdot b$, where $a$ is a component of the gate branch and $b$ of the value branch. It is worth seeing in 3D.

The gradients here are simple and instructive: $\frac{\partial g}{\partial b}=\operatorname{SiLU}(a)$ — the value branch learns in proportion to how far the gate is open; $\frac{\partial g}{\partial a}=\operatorname{SiLU}'(a)\cdot b$ — the gate learns only if there is something to pass through it.

Why the hidden size is 8/3·d

SwiGLU has three matrices instead of two. To keep the parameter count of an ordinary FFN with $h=4d$ ($2\cdot d\cdot4d=8d^2$), the hidden size is reduced to $h=\frac{2}{3}\cdot4d=\frac83 d$: then $3\cdot d\cdot\frac83d=8d^2$. For LLaMA-7B $\frac83\cdot4096\approx10923$ is rounded up to a multiple of 256: $h=11008$. That gives $3\cdot4096\cdot11008=135{,}266{,}304$ parameters per block versus $134{,}217{,}728$ for an FFN with $h=4d$ — only $0.8\%$ more.

Where it came from

Gated linear units (GLU, $\sigma(W_1x)\odot W_3x$) were proposed by Dauphin et al. (2017) for convolutional language models. Shazeer (2020) compared variants with different gates — ReGLU, GEGLU, SwiGLU — and all of them beat the ordinary FFN. SwiGLU was chosen for PaLM (2022), then for LLaMA (2023), and since then it has been the standard of open LLMs: Qwen, Mistral and many others.

SiLU in training

How does a smooth activation help in practice? A network of ReLU neurons assembles a function from line segments, a network of SiLUs from smooth “bends”. If the target dependence is smooth, SiLU neurons find it easier to reproduce. Besides, SiLU has no exact zero gradient: for a negative input the derivative is small but not zero, and the neuron does not “die”.

The experiment: a $1\to6\to1$ network approximates the curve $y=\sin1.5x+0.3x$ from 40 points. Both networks start from the same weights and differ only in the activation; training is Adam with step $0.03$ for 2000 steps.

To avoid conclusions from a single run, we repeated the experiment for eight different initializations. SiLU was more accurate in all eight, by a factor of $1.4$ to $5.2$. But this is a property of a smooth target: on a piecewise-linear dependence ReLU may do no worse. So the choice of activation is an empirical question, and in large models it is settled by experiment.

The price of smoothness

SiLU is more expensive than ReLU: it needs an exponential (or tanh), and the backward pass has to keep the input. On a GPU this is usually invisible next to the matrix multiplications, but on mobile devices people use the “hard” Swish $x\cdot\operatorname{ReLU6}(x+3)/6$ — a piecewise-polynomial approximation without exponentials.

In code

The naive form x / (1 + np.exp(-x)) at $x=-1000$ computes $e^{1000}$ and emits an overflow warning (the result is still $-0$). It is cleaner to compute the sigmoid through $\tanh$: $\sigma(x)=\tfrac12(1+\tanh\tfrac x2)$ — the tangent never overflows.

pythonimport numpy as np

def sigmoid(x):
    # σ(x) = (1 + tanh(x/2)) / 2 — no overflow for any x
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + np.tanh(0.5 * x))

def silu(x):
    return x * sigmoid(x)

def silu_grad(x):
    s = sigmoid(x)
    return s * (1.0 + x * (1.0 - s))      # = SiLU(x) + σ(x)·(1 − SiLU(x))

def swish(x, beta=1.0):
    return x * sigmoid(beta * x)

x = np.array([-1000.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.3994])
print(silu(x))        # [-0.     -0.2689  0.      0.7311  2.1997]
print(silu_grad(x))   # [-0.      0.0723  0.5     0.9277  1.0998]
pythonimport torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

z = torch.tensor([-2.0, 0.0, 2.0], requires_grad=True)
y = F.silu(z)                    # tensor([-0.2384, 0.0000, 1.7616])
y.sum().backward()
print(z.grad)                    # tensor([-0.0908, 0.5000, 1.0908])

class SwiGLU(nn.Module):
    """The FFN block of LLaMA and Qwen: W2 · (SiLU(W1 x) ⊙ W3 x)."""
    def __init__(self, d, hidden):
        super().__init__()
        self.w1 = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)   # gate branch
        self.w3 = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)   # value branch
        self.w2 = nn.Linear(hidden, d, bias=False)   # back to size d
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.w2(F.silu(self.w1(x)) * self.w3(x))

ffn = SwiGLU(4096, 11008)                          # the sizes of LLaMA-7B
print(sum(p.numel() for p in ffn.parameters()))   # 135266304
Names in libraries

PyTorch: nn.SiLU, F.silu. TensorFlow/Keras: tf.nn.silu, activation="swish" (the same function with $\beta=1$). In Hugging Face configs: hidden_act="silu". The built-in nn.init.calculate_gain does not know SiLU: people usually take the default initialization or the one for ReLU.

Common mistakes

  1. “SiLU is non-negative, like ReLU.” No: for $x<0$ it is negative, with a minimum of $-0.278$. If something that needs a non-negative input follows (a logarithm or a square root, say), this matters.
  2. “SiLU is monotonic.” No: to the left of $x\approx-1.278$ it decreases, and its derivative is negative there. It has no inverse on the whole line.
  3. Confusing SiLU and GELU. They are different functions: $x\sigma(x)$ and $x\Phi(x)$. You cannot swap one for the other in a pretrained model — the weights are tuned to a specific activation.
  4. An activation on both branches of SwiGLU. SiLU sits only on the gate branch $W_1x$; the value branch $W_3x$ is linear. An extra activation changes the model.
  5. A hidden size of $4d$ in SwiGLU. With three matrices this gives $1.5$ times more parameters than an ordinary FFN. For a fair comparison take $h\approx\frac83d$.
  6. The naive formula with exp(-x) in hand-written kernels: overflow for large negative $x$ and loss of precision in half precision. Use F.silu or the tanh form.

Links to other formulas

  • Sigmoid: the gate of SiLU; the derivative $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ enters $\operatorname{SiLU}'$.
  • ReLU: the limit of $x\sigma(\beta x)$ as $\beta\to\infty$; $\operatorname{ReLU}-\operatorname{SiLU}=|x|\sigma(-|x|)\le0.2785$.
  • GELU: $x\Phi(x)\approx x\sigma(1.702x)$ — the same self-gate with normal noise instead of logistic.
  • Leaky ReLU: another way not to zero the negative part — the line $\alpha x$ instead of a smooth dip.
  • tanh: $\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=\frac x2\bigl(1+\tanh\frac x2\bigr)$.
  • Softmax and Adam: SwiGLU’s neighbours in a transformer — the attention mechanism and the optimizer such models are trained with.

Summary

1) $\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=x\,\sigma(x)=\frac x2\bigl(1+\tanh\frac x2\bigr)$ is a self-gate: the input decides what share of itself to pass. 2) Smooth, non-monotonic, with a minimum of $-W(e^{-1})\approx-0.278$ at $\approx-1.278$; always below ReLU by at most $0.278$. 3) $\operatorname{SiLU}'=\sigma(1+x(1-\sigma))$: $0.5$ at zero, above 1 for $x>1.278$, maximum $\approx1.100$. 4) Swish-β links the linear function ($\beta=0$) and ReLU ($\beta\to\infty$); GELU $\approx$ Swish-1.702. 5) Probabilistically, it is ReLU with a random logistic threshold, averaged. 6) In LLMs it is the gate of SwiGLU: $W_2(\operatorname{SiLU}(W_1x)\odot W_3x)$ with a hidden size of $\approx\frac83d$.

Check yourself

1. What is $\operatorname{SiLU}(-1)$ approximately?
$\operatorname{SiLU}(-1)=-1\cdot\sigma(-1)=-0.2689$: the gate is about a quarter open.
2. What is the smallest value SiLU takes?
The minimum is at $x^*\approx-1.278$, with the value $x^*+1=-W(e^{-1})\approx-0.278$.
3. What is the derivative of SiLU at zero?
$\operatorname{SiLU}'(0)=\sigma(0)\bigl(1+0\bigr)=\tfrac12$.
4. In the SwiGLU block $W_2\bigl(\operatorname{SiLU}(W_1x)\odot W_3x\bigr)$, how many weight matrices are there and what activation is on the $W_3x$ branch?
The matrices are $W_1$, $W_2$, $W_3$; the value branch $W_3x$ is linear, and SiLU sits only on the gate branch.
5. What does $x\,\sigma(\beta x)$ become at $\beta=0$?
$\sigma(0)=\tfrac12$, so $x\,\sigma(0)=x/2$. As $\beta\to\infty$, on the contrary, we get ReLU.
