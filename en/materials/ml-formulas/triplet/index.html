Triplet Loss: the positive closer than the negative

A loss that learns distances rather than classes: the anchor must be closer to its positive than to the negative by at least a margin m. We take apart the formula, the three regimes of triplets, the gradients, batch-hard mining, normalization on the sphere, and how FaceNet and person re-identification (Re-ID) rest on this mathematics.

$$L(a,p,n)=\max\bigl(0,\;d(a,p)-d(a,n)+m\bigr)$$

Why we need it

An ordinary classifier knows a fixed list of classes. But a face recognition system must recognize people who were not in the training set, and a re-identification (Re-ID) system must find the same person in another camera’s footage, from another angle and under different light. Retraining the network for every new person is impossible.

The way out is to learn not classes but a metric (metric learning). The network $f$ turns an image into an embedding vector $f(x)\in\mathbb R^d$ so that pictures of the same person end up close together and pictures of different people far apart. Then “who is this?” becomes a nearest-neighbour search by distance, and a new person is added as a single embedding in the database.

Triplet Loss states this requirement directly. Take a triplet: an anchor $a$, a positive $p$ (the same person) and a negative $n$ (a different person). Require the positive to be closer than the negative by at least a margin $m$.

  • face recognition — FaceNet (Google, 2015): 128-dimensional embeddings, margin $0.2$, accuracy $99.63\%$ on the LFW benchmark;
  • re-identifying people across cameras — the batch-hard triplet loss (Hermans et al., 2017) and the torchreid library, where it complements cross-entropy when training OSNet;
  • searching for similar images, products, voices and any search by meaning where the question is “what is closest to this sample?”.
The essentials in a minute

$L=\max\bigl(0,\,d(a,p)-d(a,n)+m\bigr)$. If the negative is farther than the positive by more than $m$, the loss is zero and the triplet teaches nothing. Otherwise the gradient pulls the positive towards the anchor and pushes the negative away. Almost all random triplets are easy, so the main skill is choosing hard ones (mining), for example the farthest positive and the closest negative in the batch (batch-hard).

Intuition: the positive closer than the negative

Draw the embeddings on a plane. Around the anchor $a$ draw a circle through the positive $p$ — its radius is $d(a,p)$. Add a second one, wider by the margin: radius $d(a,p)+m$. The formula’s requirement is simple: the negative must lie outside the outer circle. If it falls into the orange ring or inside it, the loss is positive.

What to notice

The arrows at the positive and the negative always point along the lines to the anchor, and in the ordinary (non-squared) metric their lengths are equal to 1: what matters is not the size of the violation but the fact of it. Turn on squared distances and the arrow lengths become proportional to the distances.

The formula, bone by bone

Let us write the loss in full — with the network, the distances and the average over triplets:

$$\mathcal L=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{(a,p,n)\in T}\max\Bigl(0,\;\bigl\|f(x_a)-f(x_p)\bigr\|-\bigl\|f(x_a)-f(x_n)\bigr\|+m\Bigr)$$
$f(x)$
the embedding: the network output for an image $x$, a vector in $\mathbb R^d$ ($d=128$ in FaceNet), often normalized to unit length;
$d(a,p)$
the distance to the positive, $\|f(x_a)-f(x_p)\|$; we want to decrease it;
$d(a,n)$
the distance to the negative, $\|f(x_a)-f(x_n)\|$; we want to increase it;
$m>0$
the margin: the negative must be farther than the positive not just barely, but with a reserve $m$;
$\max(0,\cdot)$
the hinge: a satisfied requirement is not penalized and gives no gradient;
$T$
the set of triplets we average over — usually triplets inside a batch (see the section on mining).

It is convenient to introduce a single variable — the difference of distances $\Delta=d(a,p)-d(a,n)$. Then $L=\max(0,\Delta+m)$: a ReLU shifted by $m$. A negative $\Delta$ means the positive is already closer; but as long as $\Delta>-m$, the reserve is not enough.

Examples with $m=0.2$: $d(a,p)=0.4$, $d(a,n)=1.1$ give $\Delta=-0.7$ and $L=\max(0,\,-0.5)=0$ — the triplet is already separated. $d(a,p)=0.9$, $d(a,n)=1.0$ give $\Delta=-0.1$ and $L=0.1$: the positive is closer, but the margin is too small.

The margin is relative

Triplet Loss does not require positives to be closer than some fixed threshold — only closer than the negatives. So clusters of different people may have different sizes. The threshold for answering “this is the same person” is tuned later, on validation data.

Three regimes: easy, semi-hard, hard

Every triplet falls into one of three regimes depending on $d(a,p)$ and $d(a,n)$:

RegimeCondition$L$Gradient
easy$d(a,n)\ge d(a,p)+m$$0$none
semi-hard$d(a,p)<d(a,n)<d(a,p)+m$from $0$ to $m$yes
hard$d(a,n)\le d(a,p)$at least $m$yes

In the $(d(a,p),\,d(a,n))$ plane the regime boundaries are two parallel lines: $d(a,n)=d(a,p)$ and $d(a,n)=d(a,p)+m$. The loss is a tilted plane cut off at zero above the upper of them.

Why random triplets are useless

As soon as the network has learned a little, the vast majority of random triplets become easy: their loss and gradient are zero. Averaging over them dilutes the useful signal, and training almost stalls. Hence mining — deliberately choosing hard and semi-hard triplets.

Gradients: where each point is pulled

For an active triplet ($L>0$) the loss equals $d(a,p)-d(a,n)+m$, and everything reduces to the gradient of a distance. For the Euclidean norm $\nabla_u\|u-v\|=\frac{u-v}{\|u-v\|}$ — the unit vector from $v$ to $u$.

Deriving the gradients for the three points

Denote the embeddings $a,p,n$ and $d_{ap}=\|a-p\|$, $d_{an}=\|a-n\|$. Differentiate $d_{ap}=\sqrt{(a-p)^\top(a-p)}$ by the chain rule:

$$\nabla_a d_{ap}=\frac{1}{2d_{ap}}\cdot2(a-p)=\frac{a-p}{d_{ap}},\qquad \nabla_p d_{ap}=\frac{p-a}{d_{ap}}$$

The same holds for $d_{an}$. Substitute into $L=d_{ap}-d_{an}+m$:

$$\nabla_p L=\frac{p-a}{d_{ap}},\qquad \nabla_n L=-\frac{n-a}{d_{an}},\qquad \nabla_a L=\frac{a-p}{d_{ap}}-\frac{a-n}{d_{an}}$$

The three gradients add up to zero: shifting all three points by the same vector does not change the distances, so the loss does not change either. For squared distances $L=d_{ap}^2-d_{an}^2+m$ we get $\nabla_pL=2(p-a)$, $\nabla_nL=-2(n-a)$ and $\nabla_aL=2(n-p)$ — the anchor’s gradient does not even depend on its position.

A gradient-descent step goes against the gradient: the positive $p$ moves towards the anchor, the negative $n$ away from the anchor, and the anchor moves towards the positive and away from the negative. In the ordinary metric the lengths of these moves equal 1 at any distance; in the squared one they are proportional to the distances.

This is visible on a 3D surface. Fix the anchor at the origin and the positive at distance $0.6$; how does the loss depend on the position of the negative $n=(x,y)$? We get an upside-down cone: $L=0.6+m$ in the centre, falling to zero at the edge of a disc of radius $0.6+m$. The slope of the cone is the same everywhere, $|\nabla L|=1$.

Squared distance and hard negatives

With squared distances the repelling force $2\,d(a,n)$ is the weaker the closer the negative is to the anchor — that is, exactly for the most dangerous mistakes. In the ordinary metric it is constant. That is why in Re-ID (Hermans et al., 2017) the plain Euclidean distance is recommended; FaceNet uses squares, but on normalized vectors and with semi-hard triplets.

Mining: which triplets to take

A set of $N$ images contains on the order of $N^3$ triplets — they cannot all be enumerated, and almost all of them are easy anyway. Triplets are chosen online, inside a batch. The batch is built as $P\times K$: $P$ people with $K$ pictures each. Inside such a batch

$$|T|=\underbrace{PK}_{\text{anchors}}\cdot\underbrace{(K-1)}_{\text{positives}}\cdot\underbrace{(P-1)K}_{\text{negatives}}$$

With $P=4$, $K=4$ this is 576 triplets; with $P=18$, $K=4$ (the batch of Hermans et al. for the Market-1501 dataset) it is already 14,688. The main strategies:

  • batch-all — take all triplets of the batch and average the loss over the active ones ($L>0$) only, so that the easy ones do not dilute the signal;
  • batch-hard — one triplet per anchor: the farthest positive and the closest negative; $PK$ triplets, the most informative ones;
  • semi-hard (FaceNet) — for a pair $(a,p)$ take a negative that is farther than the positive but inside the margin; this makes training gentler at the start.
$$\mathcal L_{\text{BH}}=\frac{1}{PK}\sum_{a}\max\Bigl(0,\;\max_{p:\,y_p=y_a}d(a,p)-\min_{n:\,y_n\ne y_a}d(a,n)+m\Bigr)$$
The danger of the hardest ones

The very hardest triplets are often labelling errors or nearly indistinguishable pictures. Early in training, when the embeddings are random, chasing them can “collapse” the model: all $f(x)$ shrink to one point where $L=m$ for every triplet. Semi-hard triplets at the start, normalized embeddings, a soft margin and combining with cross-entropy all help.

How it works in training

Let us put everything together. Suppose every picture has two features: $x_1$ is a nuisance (pose, lighting) that varies a lot for the same person, and $x_2$ is an identity feature slightly bent by the nuisance. In the input space two pictures of the same person can be farther apart than pictures of different people: only $62.7\%$ of the triplets are correctly ordered.

A small network $f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ (12 tanh neurons) is trained with the batch-hard triplet loss at $m=0.5$ and the Adam optimizer. It is not told where each person should end up — only that positives must be closer than negatives.

Re-ID works the same way: the query is a picture of a person from one camera, the gallery holds pictures from all the others. The gallery embeddings are sorted by distance to the query, and one looks at the position of the first correct match. The metrics are Rank-1 (the correct match comes first) and mAP (mean average precision over all correct matches).

Where it came from

The idea of a margin between positives and negatives comes from large margin nearest neighbour (LMNN, Weinberger and Saul, 2005). Pairs of positives and negatives with a margin were introduced in the contrastive loss (Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun, 2006). Schroff, Kalenichenko and Philbin made triplets the basis of FaceNet (2015), and Hermans, Beyer and Leibe in “In Defense of the Triplet Loss for Person Re-Identification” (2017) showed that with batch-hard mining triplets work well in Re-ID too.

Normalization on the sphere and choosing the margin

Without constraints the network can increase all distances by simply stretching the space: in the ordinary metric the margin $m$ loses its meaning if the scale is not fixed. That is why embeddings are often normalized: $\hat f=f/\|f\|$, and all points lie on the unit sphere. For unit vectors

$$\|u-v\|^{2}=\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2-2\,u^\top v=2-2\cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors. The squared distance ranges from 0 (identical) to 4 (opposite), and FaceNet’s margin $m=0.2$ is $5\%$ of the whole range. The requirement $d^2(a,n)\ge d^2(a,p)+m$ becomes a requirement on angles: $\cos\theta_{an}\le\cos\theta_{ap}-m/2$.

How to choose the margin

The margin is measured in units of distance, so it depends on the normalization and on whether the distance is squared. For normalized embeddings typical values are $0.2$ (FaceNet, $d^2$) and $0.3$ (torchreid, $d$). A margin that is too small hardly demands any separation; one that is too large may be unreachable and keeps all triplets active.

In code: batch mining and stability

The key to efficiency is the matrix of all pairwise distances in the batch, from which mining is done with masks and no loops. A numerical subtlety: $\|u-v\|$ is not differentiable at $u=v$ (the derivative of the square root at zero is infinite), and the formula $\|u\|^2-2u^\top v+\|v\|^2$ may produce a tiny negative number due to rounding. So $\max(\cdot,\varepsilon)$ is put under the root, and nn.TripletMarginLoss adds $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ to the difference of vectors.

pythonimport numpy as np

def pairwise_dist(E, eps=1e-12):
    """Matrix of Euclidean distances between the rows of E (n × d)."""
    sq = (E * E).sum(1)
    d2 = sq[:, None] - 2.0 * E @ E.T + sq[None, :]
    return np.sqrt(np.maximum(d2, eps))     # max: guards against −1e-16 and the root of zero

def batch_hard_triplet(E, labels, margin=0.3):
    D = pairwise_dist(E)
    same = labels[:, None] == labels[None, :]
    hardest_pos = np.where(same, D, 0.0).max(1)       # the farthest positive
    hardest_neg = np.where(same, np.inf, D).min(1)    # the closest negative
    return np.maximum(hardest_pos - hardest_neg + margin, 0.0).mean()

E = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [0.9, 0.0], [0.6, 0.8]])   # anchor, positive, negative
labels = np.array([0, 0, 1])
print(batch_hard_triplet(E, labels, margin=0.2))      # ≈ 0.1152 = (0.1 + 0.2456 + 0) / 3
pythonimport torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

# 1) Triplets prepared in advance: the built-in loss
loss_fn = nn.TripletMarginLoss(margin=0.2, p=2)
a = torch.tensor([[0.0, 0.0]], requires_grad=True)
p = torch.tensor([[0.9, 0.0]], requires_grad=True)
n = torch.tensor([[0.6, 0.8]], requires_grad=True)
loss = loss_fn(a, p, n)            # max(0, 0.9 − 1.0 + 0.2) ≈ 0.1
loss.backward()
print(p.grad, n.grad)              # ≈ [1, 0] and [−0.6, −0.8]: unit vectors

# 2) Mining inside a P × K batch (batch-hard)
def batch_hard(emb, labels, margin=0.3, normalize=True):
    if normalize:
        emb = F.normalize(emb, dim=1)          # points on the unit sphere
    D = torch.cdist(emb, emb)                  # n × n Euclidean distances
    same = labels[:, None] == labels[None, :]
    pos = (D * same).max(1).values             # the farthest positive
    neg = D.masked_fill(same, float("inf")).min(1).values   # the closest negative
    return F.relu(pos - neg + margin).mean()
In practice

A $P\times K$ batch (for example $P=16$, $K=4$) is a must: without several pictures of the same person in the batch there are no positives. The triplet loss is usually combined with cross-entropy over identities — training is then faster and more stable.

Common mistakes

  1. Random batches instead of $P\times K$. In a random batch many anchors have no positive at all, and no triplets can be formed.
  2. Training on random triplets only. Almost all of them are easy, the gradient is zero, and the loss “freezes” at a small value although the model is poor.
  3. The hardest triplets from the very first step without normalization or warm-up — a risk of all embeddings collapsing to a point.
  4. A margin that ignores the scale. Without normalization the network stretches the space; on the sphere $m$ has to be related to the maximum distance (2 for $d$, 4 for $d^2$).
  5. The square root of zero. The distance of a point to itself on the diagonal of the matrix gives a NaN gradient unless $\varepsilon$ is added or the diagonal is masked.

Links to other formulas

The closest relative is the contrastive loss: it works with pairs and absolute thresholds rather than a relative comparison. For a pair labelled $y=1$ (same) or $y=0$ (different):

$$L_{\text{contr}}=y\,d^{2}+(1-y)\,\max(0,\;m-d)^{2}$$
  • ReLU: the hinge $\max(0,\Delta+m)$ is a ReLU of the shifted difference of distances; its derivative is a step.
  • Sigmoid: the derivative of the soft margin $\ln(1+e^\Delta)$ is $\sigma(\Delta)$.
  • Softmax and cross-entropy: the N-pair loss and InfoNCE generalize the triplet to one positive against many negatives via a softmax over similarities; ArcFace moves the margin into an angle inside the softmax.
  • MSE: the squared Euclidean distance is the same sum of squared differences.
  • Focal Loss: the same idea of “learning from the hard ones”, through a per-example weight rather than triplet selection.
  • Adam: the optimizer of the simulation in Figure 6.

Summary

1) $L=\max(0,\,d(a,p)-d(a,n)+m)$: the positive must be closer than the negative with a reserve $m$; it is a ReLU of $\Delta+m$. 2) Regimes: easy ($L=0$, no gradient), semi-hard ($0<L<m$), hard ($L\ge m$). 3) The gradients are unit vectors along the lines to the anchor (proportional to the distances for $d^2$), and they add up to zero. 4) Triplets are chosen inside a $P\times K$ batch: batch-all, batch-hard, semi-hard. 5) Normalization on the sphere gives $d^2=2-2\cos\theta$ and a meaningful margin. 6) FaceNet and Re-ID rest on this: an embedding plus nearest-neighbour search.

Check yourself

1. $m=0.2$, $d(a,p)=0.9$, $d(a,n)=1.0$. What is the loss?
$\max(0,\,0.9-1.0+0.2)=0.1$: the positive is closer, but the reserve $0.1$ is smaller than the margin $0.2$.
2. A triplet with $d(a,p)=0.5$, $d(a,n)=0.4$ at $m=0.2$ is…
The negative is closer than the positive: $d(a,n)\le d(a,p)$, a hard triplet; $L=0.3\ge m$.
3. Where does the gradient-descent step for the negative $n$ of an active triplet point (ordinary metric)?
$\nabla_nL=-(n-a)/d(a,n)$; the step goes against the gradient: $+(n-a)/d(a,n)$ — a unit vector away from the anchor.
4. What does batch-hard pick for each anchor?
The worst case for the anchor: $\max d(a,p)$ and $\min d(a,n)$ — the most informative triplet.
5. For unit vectors at an angle of $90^\circ$ the squared distance is…
$d^2=2-2\cos90^\circ=2$.
