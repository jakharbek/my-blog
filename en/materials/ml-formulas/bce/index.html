BCE: binary cross-entropy

The main loss function of binary classification. We take it apart bone by bone: where it comes from (likelihood and information theory), why together with the sigmoid its gradient is simply “prediction − truth”, why it beats MSE, what its landscape looks like in 3D, how to compute it without log(0), and how it relates to class weights, label smoothing and calibration.

$$\mathrm{BCE}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[y_i\log\hat{y}_i+(1-y_i)\log\bigl(1-\hat{y}_i\bigr)\Bigr]$$

Why we need it

A binary classifier outputs a number $\hat y\in(0,\,1)$ — its probability that an object belongs to class 1: the email is spam, the transaction is fraud, the scan shows a tumour. To learn, the model needs a loss function: a single number that grows as the prediction gets worse and can be differentiated. Binary cross-entropy (BCE; in statistics, log loss or negative log-likelihood) is the standard choice for this task.

Where it is used:

  • logistic regression and the output of any binary classifier — the pair sigmoid + BCE;
  • multi-label classification — every label (photo tags, toxicity categories) gets its own sigmoid and its own BCE, the labels are independent;
  • gradient boosting — binary:logistic in XGBoost, binary in LightGBM, Logloss in CatBoost are the same BCE on the raw score of the trees;
  • the GAN discriminator is trained with BCE: “real” is 1, “generated” is 0;
  • reward models in RLHF and DPO — the loss $-\log\sigma(r_{\text{better}}-r_{\text{worse}})$ is BCE with label 1 on the difference of scores (the Bradley–Terry model);
  • a metric — log loss in competitions and reports: it checks not only whether the model guessed right but also how honest its probabilities are.
The essentials in a minute

BCE is $-\log$ of the probability the model gave to the correct answer. A confident right answer costs almost 0, a “50/50” answer costs $\ln2\approx0.693$, a confident mistake costs arbitrarily much. Together with the sigmoid, the gradient with respect to the logit is simply $\hat y-y$: no saturation, bounded by one in magnitude and proportional to the error. BCE is convex in the weights of logistic regression, and its minimum is reached at honest probabilities.

Intuition: a penalty for surprise

Think of the model as a weather forecaster who names the probability of rain every day. If they said “90%” and it rained, they are hardly surprised — a small penalty. If they said “10%” and it rained, they were wrong and sure of it; the penalty should be large. And if they refuse to commit and always say “50%”, the penalty is the same every day — $\ln2\approx0.693$.

BCE is built exactly like that: the loss is $-\ln$ of the probability of what actually happened. For the correct answer $y=1$ it is $-\ln\hat y$ (the red curve), for $y=0$ it is $-\ln(1-\hat y)$ (the blue one). Drag the point.

What to notice

The confident mistake $\hat y=0.1$ costs 22 times more than the confident right answer $\hat y=0.9$ ($2.303$ versus $0.105$). And the loss has no ceiling: as $\hat y\to0$ the wall goes to infinity. One more property of the logarithm: every halving of the probability of the correct answer adds the same amount, $\ln2\approx0.693$, to the loss.

The formula, bone by bone

Take one term — the loss on a single object — and look at every part.

$$\ell(y,\hat y)=-\Bigl[\underbrace{y\log\hat y}_{\text{active when }y=1}+\underbrace{(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)}_{\text{active when }y=0}\Bigr]$$
$y\in\{0,1\}$
the correct answer (label). It acts as a switch: for $y=1$ the second term is multiplied by 0, for $y=0$ the first one;
$\hat y\in(0,\,1)$
the predicted probability of class 1, usually $\hat y=\sigma(z)$, where $z$ is the logit (the raw output of the model);
$1-\hat y$
the predicted probability of class 0 — the two probabilities add up to 1;
$\log$
the natural logarithm ($\ln$): the loss is measured in nats; with $\log_2$ it would be in bits;
$-$
the minus is needed because the logarithm of a probability is negative: $\log\hat y\le0$, while a loss must be $\ge0$;
$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}$
averaging over objects, so that the loss does not depend on the batch size.

The switch gives a compact form: let $p_t$ be the probability the model gave to the correct class ($p_t=\hat y$ for $y=1$ and $p_t=1-\hat y$ for $y=0$). Then

$$\ell=-\log p_t\qquad\text{and in terms of the logit }z:\qquad \ell=\log\bigl(1+e^{z}\bigr)-y\,z=\operatorname{softplus}(z)-y\,z$$

The second form, “BCE on logits”, will be needed to derive the gradient and to write stable code. If the labels are written as $s=\pm1$, the same loss becomes the logistic loss $\log(1+e^{-s z})$, where $s z$ is the margin: a positive margin means a correct answer.

An example with four objects

Labels $y=(1,\,0,\,1,\,1)$, predictions $\hat y=(0.9,\,0.2,\,0.6,\,0.95)$. The probabilities of the correct answers: $0.9$, $0.8$, $0.6$, $0.95$. The losses: $0.105$, $0.223$, $0.511$, $0.051$. The mean is $\mathrm{BCE}\approx0.223$. The third object is the main “culprit”: the model gave the correct answer only $0.6$.

Where it comes from: maximum likelihood

BCE is not made up by eye — it is derived. Suppose the model says: “the object belongs to class 1 with probability $\hat y$”. That is a Bernoulli distribution, and its formula fits on one line — again with the label as a switch, but now in the exponent:

$$P(y\mid x)=\hat y^{\,y}\,(1-\hat y)^{1-y}=\begin{cases}\hat y, & y=1\\ 1-\hat y, & y=0\end{cases}$$

If the objects are independent, the probability of seeing all the labels of the training set — the likelihood — is a product. Maximum likelihood chooses the model parameters that make this product as large as possible:

$$\mathcal L=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\hat y_i^{\,y_i}(1-\hat y_i)^{1-y_i}\;\xrightarrow{\;-\frac1n\log\;}\;-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[y_i\log\hat y_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat y_i)\Bigr]=\mathrm{BCE}$$

The logarithm turns the product into a sum and does not move the maximum (it is strictly increasing), the minus turns the maximum into a minimum, and $\tfrac1n$ turns the sum into a mean. So minimizing BCE is the same as maximizing the likelihood of a Bernoulli model.

In the example above: $\mathcal L=0.9\cdot0.8\cdot0.6\cdot0.95=0.4104$, and $-\ln0.4104\approx0.891$ is exactly the sum of the four losses; divided by 4 it is the same $\mathrm{BCE}\approx0.223$.

BCE as a geometric mean

It follows that $e^{-\mathrm{BCE}}=\mathcal L^{1/n}$ is the geometric mean of the probabilities the model gave to the correct answers. In the example $e^{-0.223}\approx0.800$: “on average the model puts 80% on the right answer”. A handy way to explain a BCE value to a non-specialist.

Why the logarithm and not the product itself

First, the product of a million probabilities below 1 becomes zero in a computer (underflow), while the sum of logarithms is an ordinary number. Second, the derivative of a sum is the sum of derivatives: the gradient over a batch adds up the contributions of the objects. Third, the logarithm is the only function (up to a factor) that turns independence (multiplying probabilities) into adding losses — the same reason it appears in the definition of information.

The information-theory view: surprise, entropy, KL

The same result comes from another direction. Claude Shannon called $I(p)=-\log p$ the surprise (self-information) of an event with probability $p$. A certain event ($p=1$) is no surprise ($I=0$); a rare one is a big surprise; the surprises of independent events add up. In bits ($\log_2$) an event with probability $\tfrac18$ carries 3 bits; in nats ($\ln$) one bit equals $\ln2\approx0.693$.

Let the real world be a coin with probability $y$ of “1”, while the model believes in the probability $\hat y$. Then:

$$\underbrace{H(y)=-y\log y-(1-y)\log(1-y)}_{\text{entropy: mean surprise when you know the truth}}$$
$$\underbrace{H(y,\hat y)=-y\log\hat y-(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)}_{\text{cross-entropy: mean surprise when you believe }\hat y}$$

The cross-entropy is exactly BCE (hence the name), and the formula also works for “soft” labels $y\in[0,\,1]$. The difference between the two is the Kullback–Leibler divergence (KL):

$$H(y,\hat y)=H(y)+\mathrm{KL}(y\,\|\,\hat y),\qquad \mathrm{KL}(y\,\|\,\hat y)=y\log\frac{y}{\hat y}+(1-y)\log\frac{1-y}{1-\hat y}\;\ge\;0$$

The entropy $H(y)$ does not depend on the model, so minimizing BCE means minimizing KL, the “distance” from the model’s beliefs to the truth. KL is zero only when $\hat y=y$. For hard labels $y\in\{0,1\}$ the entropy is zero, and BCE equals KL.

Code length

There is an engineering meaning too. An optimal code spends about $-\log_2p$ bits on an event with probability $p$. If we encode events believing in $\hat y$ instead of the true $y$, the mean message length equals the cross-entropy, and the overhead compared with the ideal is exactly KL. Training a model with BCE teaches it to “compress” the labels as economically as possible.

Proof: KL ≥ 0 (Gibbs’ inequality)

Use $\ln t\le t-1$ (the tangent to the concave logarithm at $t=1$ lies above the graph):

$$-\mathrm{KL}=y\ln\frac{\hat y}{y}+(1-y)\ln\frac{1-\hat y}{1-y}\le y\Bigl(\frac{\hat y}{y}-1\Bigr)+(1-y)\Bigl(\frac{1-\hat y}{1-y}-1\Bigr)=\hat y-y+(1-\hat y)-(1-y)=0$$

Equality in $\ln t\le t-1$ holds only at $t=1$, so $\mathrm{KL}=0$ only when $\hat y=y$ (terms with a zero weight, $y=0$ or $1-y=0$, simply drop out).

Properties

  • Non-negativity. $\ell\ge0$; for hard labels $\ell=0$ only in the limit $\hat y\to y$. With a sigmoid at the output $\hat y$ is never exactly 0 or 1, so zero is never reached.
  • Unbounded above. For $y=1$, $\hat y\to0$ the loss tends to $+\infty$. But in logits the growth is only linear: $\operatorname{softplus}(-z)\approx-z$ for $z\ll0$, so a confident mistake with logit $-10$ costs about 10, not infinity.
  • Bounded gradient in the logit. $|\partial\ell/\partial z|=|\hat y-y|<1$: each object pulls the model with a force of at most 1, however wrong the model is.
  • Convex in $\hat y$: $\dfrac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\hat y^2}=\dfrac{y}{\hat y^2}+\dfrac{1-y}{(1-\hat y)^2}>0$. Convex in the logit: $\dfrac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial z^2}=\hat y(1-\hat y)>0$. Hence convexity in the weights of logistic regression (see below).
  • Class symmetry: $\ell(z,y)=\ell(-z,1-y)$ — renaming the classes and flipping the sign of the logit changes nothing.
  • A strictly proper scoring rule: if the truth is a coin with probability $p$, the mean loss $H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$ is minimal only for the forecast $q=p$. BCE rewards honesty (see the section on calibration).
  • A baseline. A constant model that always predicts the share of positives $\pi$ gets $\mathrm{BCE}=H(\pi)$. For balanced classes this is $\ln2\approx0.693$, for $\pi=0.1$ it is $0.325$. A model worse than this threshold has learned nothing.
Convexity in the weights of logistic regression: the Hessian

Let $z_i=w^\top x_i$ (the bias $b$ is included in $w$ through a constant feature). The gradient and the Hessian of the mean BCE:

$$\nabla_w\mathrm{BCE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat y_i-y_i)\,x_i,\qquad \nabla_w^2\mathrm{BCE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat y_i(1-\hat y_i)\,x_ix_i^\top=\frac1n X^\top S X$$

where $S=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\hat y_i(1-\hat y_i)\bigr)$. For any vector $v$: $v^\top\nabla^2 v=\frac1n\sum\hat y_i(1-\hat y_i)(v^\top x_i)^2\ge0$ — the Hessian is positive semi-definite and the function is convex. So logistic regression has no bad local minima: any descent that finds a point with zero gradient has found the global minimum. The labels $y$ do not enter the Hessian at all. Newton’s method (IRLS) in classical implementations runs on this Hessian.

The derivative: why σ′ cancels

This is the central formula of the page. First the derivative with respect to the probability $\hat y$, then with respect to the logit $z$, where $\hat y=\sigma(z)$.

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\hat y}=-\frac{y}{\hat y}+\frac{1-y}{1-\hat y}=\frac{\hat y-y}{\hat y\,(1-\hat y)}$$

This derivative is awkward: as $\hat y\to0$ or $\hat y\to1$ the denominator tends to zero and the gradient explodes (the table above shows $-1000$ at $\hat y=0.001$). But the model does not output $\hat y$ directly — it outputs a logit, and the probability comes from a sigmoid. The chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z}=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\hat y}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat y}{\partial z}=\frac{\hat y-y}{\hat y\,(1-\hat y)}\cdot\underbrace{\hat y\,(1-\hat y)}_{\sigma'(z)}=\hat y-y$$

The denominator of the first fraction is exactly the derivative of the sigmoid, $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)$ (derived on the sigmoid page). They cancel, and what remains is “prediction minus truth”.

Step-by-step derivation via softplus

Rewrite the loss through the logit. Since $\log\sigma(z)=-\log(1+e^{-z})=-\operatorname{softplus}(-z)$ and $\log(1-\sigma(z))=\log\sigma(-z)=-\operatorname{softplus}(z)$:

$$\ell=y\operatorname{softplus}(-z)+(1-y)\operatorname{softplus}(z)=\operatorname{softplus}(z)-y\,z$$

Here we used the identity $\operatorname{softplus}(-z)=\log(1+e^{-z})=\log\bigl(e^{-z}(e^{z}+1)\bigr)=\operatorname{softplus}(z)-z$. The derivative of softplus is the sigmoid: $\frac{d}{dz}\log(1+e^{z})=\frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}}=\sigma(z)$. Hence

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z}=\sigma(z)-y,\qquad \frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial z^2}=\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)$$

The second derivative is positive for every $z$ — the loss is strictly convex in the logit.

Why this is no coincidence: the canonical link

The Bernoulli distribution belongs to the exponential family: $\log P(y)=y\,z-A(z)$, where $z=\log\frac{\hat y}{1-\hat y}$ is the natural parameter (the logit) and $A(z)=\log(1+e^{z})=\operatorname{softplus}(z)$ is the log-partition function. For any such family $A'(z)$ equals the mean, here $\sigma(z)$. That is why the negative log-likelihood $A(z)-yz$ always has the gradient “mean − observation”.

The sigmoid is the (inverse of the) canonical link function of the Bernoulli distribution in generalized linear models. Exactly the same picture holds for softmax + cross-entropy (gradient $s-y$) and for “identity output + MSE” with Gaussian noise (gradient $\hat y-y$). That is why “prediction − truth” keeps recurring throughout machine learning.

For the weights of logistic regression, $\hat y_i=\sigma(w^\top x_i+b)$, this gives the short formulas used in training:

$$\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{BCE}}{\partial w}=\frac1n X^\top(\hat y-y),\qquad \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{BCE}}{\partial b}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat y_i-y_i)$$
The error signal

The gradient $\hat y-y$ lies in $(-1,\,1)$, depends linearly on the error in probability and contains no factor $\sigma'(z)$. A model that is confidently wrong gets almost the maximum push; a model that is already right gets almost none. The gradient of MSE in linear regression looks exactly the same.

Why not MSE: saturation and non-convexity

Why not train a classifier with the squared error $(\hat y-y)^2$, as in regression? Let us compute the gradient with respect to the logit when $\hat y=\sigma(z)$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bigl(\sigma(z)-y\bigr)^2=2\bigl(\sigma(z)-y\bigr)\,\underbrace{\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)}_{\sigma'(z)\;\to\;0\text{ at the tails}}$$

Here the factor $\sigma'(z)$ no longer cancels with anything. If the model is confidently wrong ($y=1$, $z\ll0$), then $\sigma'(z)\approx0$ and the gradient is almost zero: the model “does not hear” its worst mistake. At $z=-4$ the BCE gradient is $-0.982$, the MSE gradient $-0.0347$, 28 times smaller; at $z=-6$ it is already 203 times smaller. The largest MSE gradient in magnitude, $-0.296$, is reached at $z=-\ln2\approx-0.693$ — MSE pushes hardest on a model that is only slightly wrong.

The second problem is non-convexity. For $y=1$ the second derivative of MSE in the logit is $-2\sigma(1-\sigma)^2(1-3\sigma)$, negative for $\sigma<\tfrac13$, i.e. for $z<-\ln2$. The loss there is concave and almost flat: summing such terms over many objects produces plateaus and saddle-like regions (Figure 5). The third problem is boundedness: MSE never exceeds 1, so “very wrong” costs almost as much as “hopelessly wrong”.

Deriving the second derivative of MSE

For $y=1$: $L=(1-\sigma)^2$, $L'=-2(1-\sigma)\sigma'=-2\sigma(1-\sigma)^2$. Differentiate the product, remembering $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$:

$$L''=-2\Bigl[\sigma'(1-\sigma)^2-2\sigma(1-\sigma)\sigma'\Bigr]=-2\sigma'(1-\sigma)(1-3\sigma)=-2\sigma(1-\sigma)^2(1-3\sigma)$$

The sign is set by the factor $1-3\sigma$: for $\sigma<\tfrac13$ (i.e. $z<-\ln2$) $L''<0$. The minimum of $L'$ is at the same point: $L'(-\ln2)=-2\cdot\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac49=-\tfrac{8}{27}\approx-0.296$.

Where MSE on probabilities is fine

As a metric, by all means: the mean square $(\hat y-y)^2$ is called the Brier score, and it is also a proper scoring rule. The trouble is not the square itself but training through a sigmoid: the gradient passes through $\sigma'$ and fades. There is a probabilistic reason as well: MSE is the likelihood of Gaussian noise, and 0/1 labels are not Gaussian.

The loss landscape in 3D

Let us look at the loss as a whole, as a function of the parameters. Take logistic regression with one feature, $\hat y=\sigma(wx+b)$, and 24 points (12 of each class). The mean loss is a function of two variables $L(w,b)$, so it can be drawn as a surface.

What the figure shows:

  • BCE is a convex bowl with a single minimum at $(1.441,\,0.149)$, where $L=0.451$. Far from the minimum its walls are almost flat inclined planes, not a parabola: the loss grows linearly and the gradient is bounded.
  • MSE has plateaus and folds. Its minimum $(1.374,\,0.320)$, $L=0.150$, lies in a narrow valley, and at the edges the surface is almost horizontal.
  • Speed of descent. At the start the gradient norm is $0.969$ for BCE and $0.0174$ for MSE, 56 times smaller. BCE gets within $0.01$ of its minimum after only 15 steps and stops (gradient norm $<10^{-4}$) at step 63. MSE gets within $0.01$ of its minimum only by step 600 and stops at step 1150; between steps 100 and 400 it crawls across a plateau where the loss barely changes ($0.500\to0.495$).
  • Different minima. The optima of the two losses do not coincide: they are different definitions of the “best” model. BCE looks for honest probabilities, MSE for the least squares of the deviations of probabilities from 0 and 1.
A cone, not a bowl

Far from the minimum the mean BCE behaves like $|w|\cdot\overline{|x|}$ — like a cone. That is why gradient descent with a constant step does not “speed up” on steep slopes, as it would on the MSE parabola of linear regression, but moves with bounded speed — another consequence of $|\hat y-y|<1$.

BCE in training

Now the two-dimensional case: 60 points of two classes, the model $\hat y=\sigma(w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b)$, full-batch gradient descent with step $\eta=1$ from zero weights:

$$w\leftarrow w-\eta\,\frac1n X^\top\bigl(\sigma(Xw+b)-y\bigr),\qquad b\leftarrow b-\eta\,\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\sigma(w^\top x_i+b)-y_i\bigr)$$

Look at the sizes of the points: the largest ones are those on the “wrong” side of the boundary. Their gradient $|\hat y-y|$ is close to 1, while for confidently and correctly classified points it is close to zero. BCE itself decides where to spend the model’s “attention”: mostly on mistakes and on points near the boundary.

Linearly separable data: the weights run off to infinity

If a line separates the classes, BCE can be made arbitrarily small just by increasing $\|w\|$: the boundary stays in place while the sigmoid gets steeper and steeper. There is no minimum — only an infimum of 0. The counter in the figure shows it: at step 100 the loss is $0.0205$ and $\|w\|=3.94$, at step 200 — $0.0133$ and $4.77$, at step 400 — $0.0082$ and $5.76$. The norm grows roughly like the logarithm of the number of steps and never stops.

Where the direction of the weights converges

On separable data the BCE gradient is governed by the points with the smallest margin: the contribution of a point decays like $e^{-\text{margin}}$. That is why the direction $w/\|w\|$ of gradient descent converges to the maximum-margin direction, the same as that of the hard-margin SVM (Soudry et al., 2018), while the norm grows like $\log t$. This is the “implicit regularization” of gradient descent. In practice the endless growth of the weights is stopped with $L_2$ regularization, early stopping or label smoothing.

Numerical stability: BCEWithLogits

The formula $-[y\log\hat y+(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)]$ is flawless on paper but breaks in a computer. If you first compute $\hat y=\sigma(z)$ and then the logarithm, three problems arise:

  1. log(0). In float32 the value $\sigma(z)$ rounds to exactly 1 already for $z>16.64$: then $1-\hat y=0$, the loss is $\infty$ and the gradient is NaN. In float64 the same starts at $z>36.74$.
  2. Loss of precision before that. The difference $1-\sigma(z)$ of two close numbers loses significant digits (catastrophic cancellation): at $z=16$ naive float32 gives $15.942$ instead of $16$, and the plot turns into steps.
  3. Clipping is a patch, not a cure. If $\hat y$ is clipped to $[\varepsilon,\,1-\varepsilon]$ with $\varepsilon=10^{-7}$ (as Keras does), the loss hits a ceiling of $-\ln10^{-7}\approx16.118$, and in float32 even $15.942$, because $1-10^{-7}$ rounds to $1-2^{-23}$. Worse, beyond the ceiling the gradient of the clip is zero: confidently wrong examples stop teaching the model. PyTorch BCELoss does something similar — it clamps the logarithm at $-100$.

The cure is never to compute $\hat y$ separately but to compute the loss directly from the logit, through softplus and the log-sum-exp trick:

$$\ell(z,y)=\operatorname{softplus}(z)-y\,z=\max(z,0)-y\,z+\log\bigl(1+e^{-|z|}\bigr)$$
Where this form comes from

We have already obtained $\ell=\operatorname{softplus}(z)-yz$. It remains to compute $\operatorname{softplus}(z)=\log(e^{0}+e^{z})$ without overflow. The log-sum-exp trick: factor out the largest exponential, $m=\max(0,z)$:

$$\log\bigl(e^{0}+e^{z}\bigr)=m+\log\bigl(e^{-m}+e^{z-m}\bigr)=\max(z,0)+\log\bigl(1+e^{-|z|}\bigr)$$

One exponent is 0 and the other is $-|z|\le0$, so the exponential never overflows, and log1p computes $\log(1+u)$ accurately for small $u$. As $|z|\to\infty$ the last term smoothly vanishes and the loss becomes exactly linear.

pythonimport numpy as np

def sigmoid(z):
    # σ(z) = (1 + tanh(z/2)) / 2 — no overflow for any z
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + np.tanh(0.5 * np.asarray(z, dtype=float)))

def bce_with_logits(z, y, pos_weight=1.0):
    """Stable BCE on logits: y·softplus(−z) + (1 − y)·softplus(z)."""
    z, y = np.asarray(z, dtype=float), np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
    sp_pos = np.logaddexp(0.0, z)      # softplus(z)  = −log(1 − σ(z))
    sp_neg = sp_pos - z                # softplus(−z) = −log σ(z)
    return np.mean(pos_weight * y * sp_neg + (1 - y) * sp_pos)

def bce_grad(z, y):
    """Gradient of the mean BCE with respect to the logits: (σ(z) − y) / n."""
    return (sigmoid(z) - y) / len(z)

z = np.array([3.0, -2.0, 0.5, 20.0])
y = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0])
print(bce_with_logits(z, y))         # 5.1624 — almost all of it comes from the last example
print(bce_grad(z, y))                # ≈ [-0.0119  0.0298 -0.0944  0.25]
print(bce_with_logits(z, y, 10.0))   # 6.3384 — positive examples weigh 10 times more

# Naive in float32: σ(20) rounds to 1, and log(1 − 1) = −inf
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.float32(20.0)))
print(-np.log(1 - p))                # inf (and a RuntimeWarning)
pythonimport torch
import torch.nn as nn

z = torch.tensor([3.0, -2.0, 0.5, 20.0], requires_grad=True)   # logits
y = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0])

loss = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()(z, y)    # σ and log inside one stable formula
loss.backward()
print(loss.item())                     # 5.1624
print(z.grad)                          # (σ(z) − y) / 4 ≈ [-0.0119, 0.0298, -0.0944, 0.2500]

# Bad: sigmoid first, then BCELoss. σ(20) in float32 equals 1,
# BCELoss clamps log at −100, and the last example costs 100 instead of 20
p = torch.sigmoid(z.detach())
print(nn.BCELoss()(p, y).item())       # 25.1624

# Class imbalance: a mistake on a positive example costs 10 times more
loss_w = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss(pos_weight=torch.tensor([10.0]))

# Label smoothing: BCEWithLogitsLoss has no such argument, so we smooth by hand
eps = 0.1
y_smooth = y * (1 - eps) + eps / 2     # 1 → 0.95, 0 → 0.05
The rule

The model returns logits, and the loss applies the sigmoid itself: BCEWithLogitsLoss in PyTorch, from_logits=True in Keras, optax.sigmoid_binary_cross_entropy in JAX. Apply the sigmoid to the output only when you need the probability itself — for a report or a threshold.

Class imbalance: weights and pos_weight

In real tasks positive examples are often rare: 1% of transactions are fraud, 0.1% of impressions are clicks. Plain BCE honestly learns probabilities, but the probability of the rare class is small almost everywhere, and with a $0.5$ threshold the model finds hardly anyone. A common remedy is a weight $w_+$ for the positive class (pos_weight in PyTorch):

$$\ell_{w}=-\Bigl[w_+\,y\log\hat y+(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\Bigr],\qquad \frac{\partial\ell_w}{\partial z}=\begin{cases}w_+(\hat y-1), & y=1\\ \hat y, & y=0\end{cases}$$

The weight $w_+$ is equivalent to repeating every positive example $w_+$ times. For a well-specified model the optimal odds are multiplied by $w_+$, i.e. the logit shifts by $\ln w_+$. A constant model for a share of positives $\pi$ chooses

$$\hat y^{*}=\frac{w_+\pi}{w_+\pi+1-\pi},\qquad w_+=\frac{1-\pi}{\pi}=\frac{n_{\text{neg}}}{n_{\text{pos}}}\;\Longrightarrow\;\hat y^{*}=\tfrac12$$

That is why the popular choice $w_+=n_{\text{neg}}/n_{\text{pos}}$ “balances” the classes: the constant becomes $0.5$.

Three conclusions from the figure:

  • Without weights the mean prediction equals the class share: $0.091=15/165$. This is no coincidence: for logistic regression with a bias, at the optimum $\partial\mathrm{BCE}/\partial b=\frac1n\sum(\hat y_i-y_i)=0$, i.e. $\sum\hat y_i=\sum y_i$.
  • The weight moves the threshold; it does not make the model smarter. With $w_+=10$ the bias $b$ shifts by $1.91$ — close to $\ln10\approx2.30$ (on a finite sample $w$ changes too). Recall goes up, precision goes down: this is the same trade-off curve you can also move along with the threshold.
  • Weights spoil calibration. The mean $\hat y=0.244$ no longer equals the share $0.091$. If you need real probabilities, train without weights and choose the threshold (it is $1/(1+w_+)$ for the same decision), or convert the odds back: $\text{odds}=\text{odds}_w/w_+$.
Deriving the optimal constant

The mean weighted loss of a constant $q$ with a share of positives $\pi$: $f(q)=-w_+\pi\ln q-(1-\pi)\ln(1-q)$. Set the derivative to zero:

$$f'(q)=-\frac{w_+\pi}{q}+\frac{1-\pi}{1-q}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;q=\frac{w_+\pi}{w_+\pi+1-\pi},\qquad \frac{q}{1-q}=w_+\cdot\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}$$

The odds are multiplied by exactly $w_+$, and the logit shifts by $\ln w_+$. With $w_+=1$ we get $q=\pi$ — the very law “the mean prediction equals the share”.

Not only weights

If the rare class is also “hard”, Focal Loss helps: the factor $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ damps easy examples. With $\gamma=0$ it coincides with BCE. Alternatives are resampling and tuning the threshold for the metric you need on validation data.

Label smoothing

With hard labels the optimal logit is infinite: for the loss on a positive example to become zero, we need $z\to+\infty$. Gradient descent faithfully goes there. On a single positive example with step $\eta=1$ from $z=0$ the logit after 10, 100 and 1000 steps is $2.26$, $4.59$, $6.91$ — it grows roughly like $\ln t$ and never stops. The model learns to be overconfident.

Label smoothing replaces hard labels with soft ones:

$$y_s=y\,(1-\varepsilon)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\qquad(1\to1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},\;\;0\to\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}),\qquad z^{*}=\ln\frac{y_s}{1-y_s}$$

Now the gradient $\sigma(z)-y_s$ vanishes at a finite point $z^*$ where $\sigma(z^*)=y_s$, and the minimal loss is not 0 but the entropy $H(y_s)$ (Figure 2). With $\varepsilon=0.1$: $y_s=0.95$, $z^*=\ln19\approx2.944$, and the same descent gives $1.97$, $2.936$, $2.944$ — the logit stops. Go back to Figure 3 and move the $\varepsilon$ slider: the curve acquires a minimum and the orange gradient crosses zero.

Smoothing acts as a regularizer of confidence: the weights do not run off to infinity, the probabilities do not stick to 0 and 1, and calibration often improves. The price is that the model will never predict more than $1-\varepsilon/2$, and part of the “dark knowledge” about class similarity is lost (Müller, Kornblith, Hinton, 2019). Calibration: BCE rewards honesty

A model is calibrated if its probabilities can be taken literally: among all objects it gave about $0.8$, class 1 occurs in about 80% of cases. This is checked with a reliability diagram: the predictions are split into bins, and in each bin the mean prediction is compared with the share of ones. The summary number is the expected calibration error:

$$\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{n_b}{n}\,\bigl|\,\overline{\hat y}_b-\overline{y}_b\,\bigr|$$

Why does BCE teach calibration? If among objects with identical features class 1 occurs with frequency $p$, the mean loss of a forecast $q$ is $H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$, and it is minimal only at $q=p$. Any embellishment — towards confidence or towards caution — costs more. Accuracy cannot do this: it does not care whether the model said $0.51$ or $0.99$.

This is the basis of temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017): for a trained network one parameter $T$ in $\hat y=\sigma(z/T)$ is fitted by minimizing BCE on a validation set. Here $k=1/T$. The order of predictions and the accuracy stay the same — only the honesty of the probabilities changes.

Why networks become overconfident

Minimizing BCE on the training set, a large network can memorize it almost perfectly — locally the same situation as “separable data”: the logits grow, the probabilities stick to 0 and 1, and on new data the model is wrong just as confidently. Hence the pairing “BCE + regularization (weight decay, label smoothing, early stopping)” and calibration on a held-out set.

Common mistakes

  1. A double sigmoid. The model has already applied sigmoid, and the loss, BCEWithLogitsLoss, applies it again. Then $\sigma(\sigma(z))\in(0.5,\,0.731)$: the model simply cannot predict class 0 with a probability above one half, and the loss on such objects never drops below $\ln2\approx0.693$.
  2. Sigmoid + BCELoss instead of BCEWithLogitsLoss. It works until the first confident example, then come $\log0$, clamping at $-100$ and a zero gradient (see the section on stability).
  3. Labels $\pm1$ instead of $\{0,1\}$. With $y=-1$ the BCE formula gives $\log\hat y-2\log(1-\hat y)$, which goes to $-\infty$ as $\hat y\to0$: the loss becomes negative and training diverges. Labels $\pm1$ have their own form, $\log(1+e^{-s z})$.
  4. BCE for mutually exclusive classes. If exactly one of $K>2$ classes is correct, you need softmax and categorical cross-entropy. Several sigmoids with BCE mean multi-label, where the classes are independent.
  5. Comparing BCE without a baseline. “A loss of 0.05” sounds great, but with 1% positives the constant gives $H(0.01)\approx0.056$ — the model has learned almost nothing. Compare with $H(\pi)$.
  6. Class weights and “probabilities”. After pos_weight the sigmoid output is no longer a probability (Figure 8). For decisions with error costs, tune the threshold; for honest probabilities, convert or calibrate.
  7. reduction='sum' instead of the mean. The sum grows with the batch size, and so does the effective step size. Change the batch size and the optimal learning rate changes too.

Links to other formulas

  • Sigmoid: turns the logit into $\hat y$; its derivative $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ cancels in the BCE gradient.
  • Categorical cross-entropy: the generalization to $K$ classes; for $K=2$ softmax + CE coincides with sigmoid + BCE of the logit difference $z_1-z_2$.
  • Softmax: for two classes $\operatorname{softmax}(z_1,z_2)_1=\sigma(z_1-z_2)$.
  • Focal Loss: $-(1-p_t)^\gamma\log p_t$ — BCE that damps easy examples; with $\gamma=0$ it equals BCE.
  • MSE: the right loss for Gaussian noise; with a sigmoid it brings saturation and non-convexity (Figures 4 and 5).
  • SGD and Adam: optimizers that use the gradient $\frac1nX^\top(\hat y-y)$.

Summary

1) BCE is $-\log$ of the probability of the correct answer; the label $y$ acts as a switch between $-\log\hat y$ and $-\log(1-\hat y)$. 2) It follows from the maximum likelihood of a Bernoulli model and equals the cross-entropy: $H(y)+\mathrm{KL}(y\,\|\,\hat y)$. 3) Paired with the sigmoid, $\partial\ell/\partial z=\hat y-y$: the factor $\sigma'$ cancels, and the gradient is bounded and does not fade on a confident mistake — unlike MSE. 4) BCE is convex in the logit and in the weights of logistic regression; far from the minimum it grows linearly. 5) Compute it from logits: $\max(z,0)-yz+\log(1+e^{-|z|})$. 6) pos_weight shifts the logit by roughly $\ln w_+$, smoothing makes the optimum finite, and the minimum of BCE is reached at calibrated probabilities.

Check yourself

1. The correct answer is $y=1$, the model output $\hat y=0.25$. What is the loss?
$-\ln0.25=\ln4\approx1.386$. For $y=1$ only the branch $-\ln\hat y$ is active.
2. The logit is $z=1.2$ and the correct answer is $y=0$. What is the derivative of BCE with respect to $z$?
$\partial\ell/\partial z=\sigma(z)-y=\sigma(1.2)-0\approx0.769$: the model is too sure of class 1, so the logit is pushed down.
3. Why does sigmoid + MSE learn poorly when the model is confidently wrong?
$\partial(\sigma-y)^2/\partial z=2(\sigma-y)\sigma'(z)$, and for $|z|\gg1$ $\sigma'(z)\to0$. In BCE this factor cancels and the gradient is $\hat y-y$.
4. 10% of the sample is positive. What loss does the best constant model get?
The best constant is $\hat y=0.1$, and its loss is the entropy $-0.1\ln0.1-0.9\ln0.9\approx0.325$. This is the baseline for any model on this data.
5. Label smoothing $\varepsilon=0.2$, label $y=1$. Which logit does training converge to?
$y_s=1-0.2/2=0.9$, and the gradient $\sigma(z)-0.9$ is zero at $z^*=\ln\frac{0.9}{0.1}=\ln9\approx2.197$.
