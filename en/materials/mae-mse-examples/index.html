$$\begin{gathered}\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl|y_i-\hat{y}_i\bigr|\\[6pt]\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i-\hat{y}_i\bigr)^2\end{gathered}$$

How to read the formulas

Both metrics answer the question «how wrong is the model?». There are $n$ objects; for each we know the correct answer $y_i$ and the model's prediction $\hat y_i$ (read «y hat»). The difference $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ is the error on object $i$.

$y_i$
the correct answer (ground truth) for object $i$
$\hat y_i$
the model's prediction for object $i$
$e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$
the error: plus means the model undershot, minus means it overshot
$n$
the number of objects
$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$
the sum over all objects from $1$ to $n$

A four-step recipe

  1. Write out the pairs $(y_i,\ \hat y_i)$ and compute the errors $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$.
  2. For MAE take the absolute value of each error $|e_i|$; for MSE take the square $e_i^2$.
  3. Add up all the absolute values (or all the squares).
  4. Divide the sum by $n$: that is the MAE (or the MSE).
How to read the answer

MAE is the average size of a miss in the same units as $y$: «on average we are off by 0.83». MSE is the average squared miss, in «squared» units; it reacts more strongly to large errors. For both metrics, smaller is better and zero is perfect.

Derivatives for backpropagation

A neural network learns like this: it computes the loss (say, MSE), then walks backwards, from the loss to the weights, multiplying derivatives layer by layer with the chain rule. This pass is called backpropagation. The very first link of the chain is the derivative of the loss with respect to the prediction $\hat y_i$: it says in which direction and how strongly to move each prediction. If you are just getting to know MAE and MSE, you can skip this section and come back to it later.

$g_i$
$g_i$ is the derivative of the loss with respect to the prediction $\hat y_i$, a component of the gradient $\nabla_{\hat y} L = (g_1, \dots, g_n)$

The derivative of MSE

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{MSE}}{\partial \hat y_i} &= \frac{1}{n}\cdot 2\,(y_i-\hat y_i)\cdot(-1)\\ &= \frac{2}{n}\,(\hat y_i - y_i)\end{aligned}$$

Of the whole sum, only one term depends on $\hat y_i$: $(y_i - \hat y_i)^2$. The derivative of a square is «two times the expression», and the derivative of $y_i - \hat y_i$ with respect to $\hat y_i$ is $-1$ (the chain rule). Hence the sign: $(\hat y_i - y_i)$, not $(y_i - \hat y_i)$.

The derivative of MAE

$$\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{MAE}}{\partial \hat y_i} = \frac{1}{n}\operatorname{sign}(\hat y_i - y_i)$$
$$\operatorname{sign}(x)=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1, & x>0\\\phantom{-}0, & x=0\\-1, & x<0\end{cases}$$

The derivative of $|x|$ is $1$ to the right of zero and $-1$ to the left. At $x = 0$ itself the absolute value has a kink and no derivative; in practice we take $0$, one of the admissible values (a subgradient), as PyTorch and NumPy do.

How to read the sign

Plus means the prediction is too high and descent will lower it; minus means it is too low and descent will raise it; zero means the object stays put. A gradient descent step: $\hat y_i \leftarrow \hat y_i - \eta\, g_i$, where $\eta$ is the learning rate. The key difference between the metrics: with MSE the strength of the signal is proportional to the error, with MAE it is always $\tfrac{1}{n}$.

In a real network the predictions are not moved directly; the weights are. The gradient with respect to the predictions is the signal that travels back to the weights; how exactly is shown in the section «From the prediction to the weights». Example 1★☆☆

The example from the board

$y = (3,\ 5,\ 8)$,   $\hat y = (2.5,\ 5,\ 10)$. Find MAE and MSE.

Solution and answer

1. The error of each object $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ and its absolute value:

$$\begin{aligned}\lvert e_{1}\rvert &= \lvert 3-2.5\rvert = \lvert 0.5\rvert = 0.5\\\lvert e_{2}\rvert &= \lvert 5-5\rvert = \lvert 0\rvert = 0\\\lvert e_{3}\rvert &= \lvert 8-10\rvert = \lvert -2\rvert = 2\end{aligned}$$

2. Sum of the absolute values:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lvert e_i\rvert = 0.5 + 0 + 2 = 2.5$$

3. MAE: divide the sum by $n = 3$:

$$\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot 2.5 = \frac{2.5}{3} \approx 0.83$$

4. The square of each error:

$$\begin{aligned}e_{1}^2 &= (3-2.5)^2 = 0.5^2 = 0.25\\e_{2}^2 &= (5-5)^2 = 0^2 = 0\\e_{3}^2 &= (8-10)^2 = (-2)^2 = 4\end{aligned}$$

5. Sum of the squares:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{3}e_i^2 = 0.25 + 0 + 4 = 4.25$$

6. MSE: divide the sum by $n = 3$:

$$\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot 4.25 = \frac{4.25}{3} \approx 1.42$$

7. The derivative of MAE with respect to each prediction: $g_i = \frac{1}{n}\operatorname{sign}(\hat y_i - y_i)$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_{1} &= \frac{\operatorname{sign}(2.5-3)}{3} \approx -0.33\\g_{2} &= \frac{\operatorname{sign}(5-5)}{3} = 0\\g_{3} &= \frac{\operatorname{sign}(10-8)}{3} \approx 0.33\end{aligned}$$

8. The derivative of MSE with respect to each prediction: $g_i = \frac{2}{n}(\hat y_i - y_i)$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_{1} &= \frac{2\cdot(2.5-3)}{3} \approx -0.33\\g_{2} &= \frac{2\cdot(5-5)}{3} = 0\\g_{3} &= \frac{2\cdot(10-8)}{3} \approx 1.33\end{aligned}$$

The gradient vectors with respect to the predictions ($\nabla = \nabla_{\hat y}$):

$$\begin{gathered}\nabla\mathrm{MAE} \approx (-0.33,\ 0,\ 0.33)\\[4pt]\nabla\mathrm{MSE} \approx (-0.33,\ 0,\ 1.33)\end{gathered}$$

MAE sends every miss a signal of the same strength $\pm\tfrac{1}{n}$ = ±0.33: only the direction of the error matters. MSE pulls object 3 the hardest: its signal is 1.33 in absolute value because it has the largest error (2).

What the derivatives show

Object 3 (error 2) gets a signal of 1.33 from MSE, four times stronger than object 1 (0.33) with an error of 0.5. With MAE both signals have the same absolute value, 0.33: the absolute value does not tell «a little» from «a lot».

Answer: $\mathrm{MAE} \approx 0.83$,   $\mathrm{MSE} \approx 1.42$

What the example shows

Two of the three errors are small and one equals 2. MAE ≈ 0.83: «on average we are off by less than one». MSE ≈ 1.42 is larger than MAE: the square of the error 2 is 4, and it pulls the average up.

Derivatives for backpropagation

7. The derivative of MAE with respect to each prediction: $g_i = \frac{1}{n}\operatorname{sign}(\hat y_i - y_i)$:

$$\begin{aligned}g_{1} &= \frac{\operatorname{sign}(10-10)}{3} = 0\\g_{2} &= \frac{\operatorname{sign}(20-20)}{3} = 0\\g_{3} &= \frac{\operatorname{sign}(36-30)}{3} \approx 0.33\end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{gathered}\nabla\mathrm{MAE} \approx (0,\ 0,\ 0.33)\\[4pt]\nabla\mathrm{MSE} = (0,\ 0,\ 4)\end{gathered}$$

From the prediction to the weights: the chain rule

Take the simplest neuron with no activation function: $\hat y_i = w\,x_i + b$. The inputs are $x = (1,\ 2,\ 3)$, the correct answers $y = (3,\ 5,\ 8)$, the current weights $w = 2$, $b = 0$, so the predictions are $\hat y = (2,\ 4,\ 6)$. How does the gradient with respect to the predictions turn into a gradient with respect to the weights?

Each prediction depends on $w$ through the factor $x_i$ and on $b$ with the factor $1$: $\frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial w} = x_i$, $\frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial b} = 1$. By the chain rule:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y_i}\cdot\frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial w} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} g_i\,x_i\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} g_i\end{aligned}$$

With MSE

The gradient with respect to the predictions:

$$\nabla_{\hat y}\mathrm{MSE} \approx (-0.67,\ -0.67,\ -1.33)$$

The gradient with respect to the weights:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{MSE}}{\partial w} &= (-\tfrac{2}{3})\cdot 1 + (-\tfrac{2}{3})\cdot 2\\ &\quad + (-\tfrac{4}{3})\cdot 3 = -6\\[6pt]\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{MSE}}{\partial b} &= (-\tfrac{2}{3}) + (-\tfrac{2}{3}) + (-\tfrac{4}{3})\\ &= -\frac{8}{3} \approx -2.67\end{aligned}$$

A descent step with $\eta = 0.1$:

$$\begin{aligned}w &\leftarrow 2 - 0.1\cdot (-6) = 2.6\\b &\leftarrow 0 - 0.1\cdot (-\tfrac{8}{3}) = \tfrac{4}{15} \approx 0.27\end{aligned}$$

The loss after the step: MSE 2 → 0.08.

With MAE

The gradient with respect to the predictions:

$$\nabla_{\hat y}\mathrm{MAE} \approx (-0.33,\ -0.33,\ -0.33)$$

The gradient with respect to the weights:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{MAE}}{\partial w} &= (-\tfrac{1}{3})\cdot 1 + (-\tfrac{1}{3})\cdot 2\\ &\quad + (-\tfrac{1}{3})\cdot 3 = -2\\[6pt]\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{MAE}}{\partial b} &= (-\tfrac{1}{3}) + (-\tfrac{1}{3}) + (-\tfrac{1}{3})\\ &= -1\end{aligned}$$

A descent step with $\eta = 0.1$:

$$\begin{aligned}w &\leftarrow 2 - 0.1\cdot (-2) = 2.2\\b &\leftarrow 0 - 0.1\cdot (-1) = 0.1\end{aligned}$$

The loss after the step: MAE ≈ 1.33 → ≈ 0.83.

With MSE the derivative with respect to $w$ is −6, with MAE it is −2: the third object is off by 2, and MSE «shouts» louder, so the step is larger. This is how one and the same error signal travels back from the loss to the weights; in a deep network the chain is simply longer: the signal is multiplied by the derivative of every layer.

Common mistakes

  • Forgot the absolute value. The errors −2 and +2 «cancel» to zero, yet the model was wrong both times (examples 5 and 15).
  • Squared a negative number incorrectly. $(-3)^2 = 9$, not $-9$: the square of any number is non-negative (example 8).
  • Divided by the wrong $n$. Divide by the number of objects, not by the number of nonzero errors (example 7).
  • Averaged first and squared afterwards. MSE is the mean of the squares, not the square of the mean: for the errors −5 and +5, MSE = 25, while the «square of the mean error» would be 0.
  • Compared MAE and MSE «by size». They have different units: with errors below 1, MSE is smaller than MAE; with large errors it is larger (examples 6 and 3).
  • Swapped the order of subtraction. That is harmless: $|y - \hat y| = |\hat y - y|$ and $(y - \hat y)^2 = (\hat y - y)^2$. Still, the signed error is conventionally $y_i - \hat y_i$.
  • Got the sign of the derivative wrong. $\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat y_i}(y_i - \hat y_i)^2 = 2(y_i - \hat y_i)\cdot(-1) = 2(\hat y_i - y_i)$: the inner derivative brings a minus. With the wrong sign, descent goes uphill and the loss grows.
  • Forgot the factor $\tfrac{2}{n}$ or $\tfrac{1}{n}$. The direction does not change, but the step becomes $n$ (or $2n$) times larger, and so does the suitable learning rate.
  • Looking for the derivative of MAE at zero. There is none: the absolute value has a kink. A subgradient is used instead, usually $0$, as in PyTorch.

The squared errors are $1$ and $9$, the sum is $10$; divide by $2$: MSE = 5.
3. All errors were tripled. How many times larger does the MSE get?
Every square grows $3^2 = 9$ times, so their mean grows 9 times as well (MAE would triple).
4. Which single number $c$ minimizes the MAE for the data $(1,\ 2,\ 10)$?
The median minimizes MAE, and the median of $1, 2, 10$ is $2$. The mean $\frac{13}{3}$ would minimize the MSE.
5. The model's errors are $0.1,\ 0.2,\ 0.3$. Which is larger?
All errors are below 1, so their squares are even smaller: MAE = 0.2, MSE ≈ 0.0467.

With MAE the gradient component is $\pm\tfrac{1}{n}$ and depends only on the sign of the error. With MSE it would grow 10 times.
