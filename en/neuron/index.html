What a single neuron does

Every neural network, from logistic regression to GPT, is assembled from one and the same part. This part is called an artificial neuron, and all of its work fits into a single line:

$$a = f\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b\Big)$$

The same formula is also written more compactly, in vector form:

$$a = f\big(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b\big)$$

These are not two different formulas but one and the same, written in two languages: the per-coordinate one and the vector one. Below we will take both apart and prove that they are equal. For now, let us read the formula as a recipe of three steps:

  1. Weigh. Multiply each input $x_i$ by its own weight $w_i$. The weight says how important the input is and whether it is “for” or “against”.
  2. Add up and shift. Add all the products and add the bias $b$. The result is a single number, the weighted sum $z$.
  3. Decide. Pass $z$ through the activation function $f$. The result is the neuron’s output $a$.
An analogy

A bank’s credit committee decides whether to grant a loan. Each expert brings their own opinion: these are the inputs $x_i$. Each expert has their own authority and their own stance: these are the weights $w_i$. A large positive weight is a respected supporter, a negative one is a sceptic, a zero weight is someone nobody listens to. The bias $b$ is the committee’s mood before the discussion begins: a strict committee starts in the minus. The function $f$ is the procedure that turns the final score into a decision: “yes/no” or “probability of default 33%”.

Where the formula came from

In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts described a “logical neuron”: it sums binary inputs and fires if the sum exceeds a threshold. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt proposed the perceptron, a neuron whose weights are fitted from examples (the Mark I hardware machine followed soon after). The image is borrowed from biology: dendrites receive signals (inputs), synapses amplify or attenuate them (weights), the cell body sums them, and the axon emits a pulse if the total excitation is above a threshold (activation). The analogy is loose: a living neuron is far more complex, but the idea “weigh, add up, decide” has survived.

The formula bone by bone: every symbol

Let us read the expression from left to right. It contains seven objects, and each has its own role, its own type and its own source: some come from the data, others are learned, and still others are chosen by the network’s architect.

$x_i$
input number $i$: one feature of the object (the floor area of a flat, the brightness of a pixel, the output of a neuron in the previous layer);
$w_i$
weight of input $i$: how strongly and in which direction this input affects the result; learned;
$i,\ n$

$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$

$b$

$z$

$f$

$a$

Inputs $x_i$: what the neuron sees

Inputs are just numbers. A neuron knows nothing about flats, images or words: all of these are first turned into numbers. For a flat, $x_1 = 60$ (floor area, m²) and $x_2 = 2$ (rooms); for a 28×28 black-and-white image, $n = 784$ pixel brightnesses; for a neuron deep inside a network, the inputs are the outputs of the neurons in the previous layer.

Inputs are not learned: they are data and come from outside. But their scale matters. If $x_1$ is income in Uzbek soums (hundreds of thousands) and $x_2$ is the number of children (single digits), the first input will overwhelm the second even before training. That is why features are usually standardised: subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation.

Weights $w_i$: what matters to the neuron

A weight is the multiplier of an input. It encodes three things at once:

The sign of a weight is the direction of the influence, its absolute value is the strength. A weight also carries units: if $x_i$ is a floor area in m² and $a$ is a price in thousands of dollars, then $w_i$ is measured in “thousands of dollars per m²”. Weights are the neuron’s memory: everything it has learned from the data is stored in them and in the bias.

The index $i$, the number of inputs $n$ and the summation sign

The $\sum$ sign is a compact notation for a long addition. Below it you write the value the counter $i$ starts from, above it the value it ends at. Let us expand the sum for $n = 3$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{3} w_i x_i = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + w_3x_3$$

The number $n$ is set by the data: the neuron has as many inputs, and as many weights, as the object has features. The order of the terms does not matter; what matters is the pairing: the $i$-th weight is always multiplied by the $i$-th input. If you shuffle the weights relative to the inputs, you get a different neuron.

The bias $b$: the initial inclination

The bias is added to the sum once and is not multiplied by anything. It is the value of $z$ when all inputs are zero, that is, the neuron’s “default” opinion before it has looked at the data. Without it the neuron loses an important degree of freedom; section 6 is about this.

The weighted sum $z$

It is convenient to give everything inside $f$ a single letter. Then the neuron’s formula splits into two stages, a linear one and a non-linear one:

$$z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b, \qquad a = f(z)$$

$z$ has many names: pre-activation, weighted sum, net input, and in classification, the logit. It is a single number of any sign and any magnitude. The first stage gathers the “evidence”, the second delivers the “verdict”.

The activation function $f$ and the output $a$

$f$ takes one number and returns one number. Its choice determines the neuron’s “profession”: the identity $f(z) = z$ turns it into linear regression, the sigmoid into a probability estimator, the step into a “yes/no” switch, ReLU into a feature detector in a hidden layer. More on this in section 7. The output $a$ is either the answer of the whole model or one of the inputs $x$ for the neurons of the next layer.

How many parameters a neuron has

There are exactly $n + 1$ trainable parameters: $n$ weights and one bias. A neuron that looks at a 28×28 image has $784 + 1 = 785$ parameters. Everything else in the formula is data ($x_i$), intermediate results ($z$, $a$) or the architect’s decision ($f$, $n$).

Step by step with numbers

Let us take a concrete neuron with three inputs and work through the formula by hand. The same example will accompany us to the end of the article.

$$\mathbf{x} = (2,\ -1,\ 3), \qquad \mathbf{w} = (0.5,\ -1,\ 0.25), \qquad b = -0.75, \qquad f = \sigma$$

Step 1. Weigh: the products

$$\begin{aligned} w_1x_1 &= 0.5\cdot 2 = 1\\ w_2x_2 &= (-1)\cdot(-1) = 1\\ w_3x_3 &= 0.25\cdot 3 = 0.75 \end{aligned}$$

Look at the second term: a negative weight and a negative input produced a positive contribution. “Against” multiplied by “less than usual” is “for”. The sign of a contribution is determined by the product of the signs, not by the sign of the weight.

Step 2. Add up

$$\sum_{i=1}^{3} w_ix_i = 1 + 1 + 0.75 = 2.75$$

Step 3. Shift

$$z = 2.75 + (-0.75) = 2$$

Step 4. Activate

$$a = \sigma(2) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.1353} \approx 0.8808$$

The neuron output $0.8808$. If it is a classifier, it is 88% confident that the object belongs to class 1. The same pre-activation $z = 2$ with other activation functions would give entirely different answers:

What will the neuron output if the third input becomes zero? The third term disappears: $z = 1 + 1 + 0 - 0.75 = 1.25$, and $a = \sigma(1.25) \approx 0.7773$. The confidence dropped because a contribution “for” of size $0.75$ is gone.

The vector form: the same formula in one move

Writing out a sum of 784 terms is inconvenient. Linear algebra offers a compact notation: collect the inputs and the weights into vectors, ordered columns of numbers.

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}w_1\\w_2\\\vdots\\w_n\end{pmatrix}$$

A bold letter is a vector, a plain one is a number. By convention vectors are columns of size $n\times 1$. The symbol $\top$ denotes transposition: it lays the column on its side and turns it into a $1\times n$ row. Multiply the row by the column using the “row times column” rule, that is, multiply pairwise and add up:

$$\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix}w_1 & w_2 & \cdots & w_n\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix} = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_nx_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_ix_i$$

We get exactly what appears in the per-coordinate formula. This is the dot product of vectors. Hence:

$$a = f\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_ix_i + b\Big) = f\big(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b\big)$$

Dimensions: why the result is a number

$$\underbrace{\mathbf{w}^{\top}}_{1\times n}\;\underbrace{\mathbf{x}}_{n\times 1} \;+\; \underbrace{b}_{1\times 1} \;=\; \underbrace{z}_{1\times 1}$$

The rule of matrix multiplication: the inner dimensions ($n$ and $n$) must match, the outer ones ($1$ and $1$) give the size of the result. Hence a common mistake: if you move the transposition and write $\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}^{\top}$, the dimensions become $(n\times 1)(1\times n) = n\times n$, and you get a table of all pairwise products (the outer product) rather than a number.

With our numbers

$$\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5 & -1 & 0.25\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix} = 0.5\cdot2 + (-1)(-1) + 0.25\cdot3 = 2.75$$

The same three products and the same sum as in section 3. The vector notation does not change the computation; it changes only the way it is written.

The geometric meaning: a neuron measures similarity

The dot product has a second, geometric formula:

$$\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\cos\theta$$

Here $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = \sqrt{w_1^2 + \dots + w_n^2}$ is the length of the vector, and $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{w}$ and $\mathbf{x}$. So the neuron compares the input with the weight vector as with a template: if $\mathbf{x}$ points the same way as $\mathbf{w}$ ($\theta < 90^\circ$), the contribution is positive; if it is perpendicular, zero; if it points the opposite way, negative. The weights are the “pattern” the neuron looks for in the input.

With our numbers: $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = \sqrt{0.25 + 1 + 0.0625} = \sqrt{1.3125} \approx 1.1456$, $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 9} = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.7417$, and $\cos\theta = 2.75 / (1.1456\cdot 3.7417) \approx 0.6415$, that is, $\theta \approx 50.1^\circ$. The input clearly resembles the template but does not coincide with it.

Why $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\cos\theta$

Consider the triangle with sides $\mathbf{w}$, $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{x}$. By the law of cosines

$$\lVert\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 - 2\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\cos\theta$$

On the other hand, expand the squared length in coordinates:

$$\lVert\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_i - x_i)^2 = \sum w_i^2 + \sum x_i^2 - 2\sum w_ix_i = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$$

The right-hand sides are equal, so $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\cos\theta$. The algebraic and the geometric formulas are one and the same.

When to use which form

The per-coordinate form shows what happens to each input: you need it to understand a feature’s contribution and to derive the derivative with respect to a single weight. The vector form shows what happens to the whole input at once: you need it for short derivations, for geometry and for fast code, since CPUs and GPUs multiply vectors and matrices “wholesale”, hundreds of times faster than a loop over elements.

Geometry: how a neuron divides space

Let us look at the neuron as a device that cuts the input space into two parts. Take two inputs: each object is a point $(x_1,\ x_2)$ in the plane. The points where the neuron is “least sure” are those where $z = 0$:

$$w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b = 0$$

This is the equation of a straight line. On one side of it $z > 0$, on the other $z < 0$. With a sigmoid it is the boundary $a = 0.5$, with a step it is the boundary between the answers “0” and “1”. In $n$ dimensions, $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ is a hyperplane: a line in the plane, a plane in three-dimensional space, and so on.

  • The vector $\mathbf{w}$ is perpendicular to the boundary and points in the direction in which $z$ grows. A one-line proof: if $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}'$ lie on the boundary, then $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}' + b = 0$, whence $\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}') = 0$: the vector $\mathbf{w}$ is perpendicular to any segment along the boundary.
  • The length $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ is the steepness. The longer $\mathbf{w}$ is, the faster $z$ grows as you move away from the boundary, and the sharper the sigmoid’s transition from 0 to 1.
  • The bias $b$ shifts the boundary parallel to itself. The distance from the origin to the boundary is $|b| / \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$.
  • $z$ is the signed distance multiplied by $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$. The sign tells which side the point is on, the absolute value tells how far away it is, that is, how confident the neuron is.
$$d(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert} = \frac{z}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}$$

For our three-dimensional example the boundary is the plane $0.5x_1 - x_2 + 0.25x_3 - 0.75 = 0$. The point $\mathbf{x} = (2,\ -1,\ 3)$ lies on its positive side at a distance $d = 2 / 1.1456 \approx 1.746$ from it.

The three-dimensional picture

Add a third axis, the output. Over the input plane, $z$ is a tilted plane, $\sigma(z)$ is a “step-shaped carpet” bent across the boundary, and $\mathrm{ReLU}(z)$ is a sheet folded along the boundary. In all three cases the output is constant along the boundary: the neuron senses only one direction, the direction of $\mathbf{w}$.

The main takeaway

A single neuron is a linear classifier. However monotonic $f$ may be, the decision boundary remains a straight line (a plane, a hyperplane). This is both the neuron’s strength, simplicity and interpretability, and its limit: problems where the classes cannot be separated by a straight line are beyond it (case 3, XOR).

The bias b: why we need the free term

Remove $b$ from the formula and plug in a zero input: $z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{0} = 0$ for any weights. The boundary $z = 0$ is forced to pass through the origin, like the line $y = kx$ without a free term, which can only be rotated around zero. The bias removes this constraint.

With a single input everything is especially clear. The boundary $wx + b = 0$ is the point

$$x_0 = -\frac{b}{w}$$

The weight $w$ is responsible for the steepness (and, when $w < 0$, for the direction), the bias $b$ for the position of the threshold. With $w = 2$, $b = -2$ the sigmoid passes through $0.5$ at the point $x_0 = 1$ rather than at zero.

A trick: the bias as one more weight

Add a dummy input $x_0 = 1$ that always equals one, and call the bias the weight $w_0 = b$. Then the bias hides inside the sum, and the sum starts from zero:

$$z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_ix_i + b = \sum_{i=0}^{n} w_ix_i = \tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\x_1\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix}, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{w}} = \begin{pmatrix}b\\w_1\\\vdots\\w_n\end{pmatrix}$$

With our numbers: $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (-0.75,\ 0.5,\ -1,\ 0.25)$, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (1,\ 2,\ -1,\ 3)$, $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = -0.75 + 2.75 = 2$. This is how it is written in statistics (a column of ones in the feature matrix) and in classic texts on the perceptron. It is convenient for derivations, but in code the bias is usually stored separately.

The bias as a threshold

With a step activation the neuron fires when $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b \ge 0$, that is, when $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} \ge -b$. So $\theta = -b$ is the firing threshold. A committee with $b = -2$ is sceptical: to say “yes”, the arguments must score more than two points. A committee with $b = +1$ is lenient: it will say “yes” even if there are no arguments at all.

Why non-linearity is needed at all

If $f$ is the identity, the neuron is a linear function of its input. But a composition of linear functions is again linear. However many layers without activations we stack one after another, the whole network collapses into a single layer with different weights and cannot learn anything that a single neuron cannot.

Proof: two linear layers equal one

Let the first layer compute $\mathbf{h} = W_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1$ and the second $\mathbf{y} = W_2\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_2$. Substitute:

$$\mathbf{y} = W_2(W_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2 = \underbrace{(W_2W_1)}_{W}\,\mathbf{x} + \underbrace{(W_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2)}_{\mathbf{b}}$$

We get a single linear layer with the matrix $W = W_2W_1$ and the bias $\mathbf{b} = W_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2$. By induction the same holds for any number of layers.

A non-linear $f$ “breaks” the straight line. From many such broken pieces a network assembles a function of practically any shape. This is what the universal approximation theorem states: a network with one hidden layer and a non-linear (more precisely, non-polynomial, like the sigmoid or ReLU) activation is enough to approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain to arbitrary accuracy (given enough neurons).

Activation pitfalls

The sigmoid and tanh saturate: for $|z| > 5$ their derivative is almost zero, and the gradient barely passes through such a neuron (see the article Anatomy of the sigmoid for details). ReLU outputs zero with a zero derivative for $z < 0$: a neuron that receives a negative $z$ on all the data stops learning, it “dies” (see ReLU and Leaky ReLU).

Cases: one formula, four professions

The formula is one, but the meaning of $x$, $w$, $b$ and the choice of $f$ turn the neuron into completely different tools. Let us go through four cases, each with numbers.

Case 1. Regression: the price of a flat ($f$ is the identity)

Inputs: $x_1$ is the floor area, m²; $x_2$ is the number of rooms; $x_3$ is the distance to the city centre, km. The output is the price in thousands of dollars. Suppose that after training the weights are (the numbers are illustrative) $\mathbf{w} = (1.2,\ 5,\ -2)$, $b = 10$.

  • $w_1 = 1.2$: each square metre adds 1,200 dollars;
  • $w_2 = 5$: an extra room with the same floor area adds 5,000 dollars;
  • $w_3 = -2$: each kilometre from the centre takes away 2,000 dollars;
  • $b = 10$: a base premium. It is not “the price of a flat with zero floor area” (there is no such flat in the data) but a correction with which the line best fits the real examples.
$$a = 1.2\cdot 60 + 5\cdot 2 + (-2)\cdot 5 + 10 = 72 + 10 - 10 + 10 = 82$$

A 60 m² flat with two rooms, 5 km from the centre: the model values it at 82 thousand dollars. A single neuron without an activation is exactly linear regression. It is trained by minimising the mean squared error (see MAE and MSE by example).

Case 2. Classification: the probability of default ($f$ is the sigmoid)

A bank’s scoring model. Inputs: $x_1 = 0.4$ is the debt-to-income ratio (the share of income that goes to loan payments); $x_2 = 2$ is the number of late payments in the past year; $x_3 = 5$ is the length of the credit history, in years. Weights: $\mathbf{w} = (3,\ 0.8,\ -0.3)$, $b = -2$.

$$\begin{aligned} z &= 3\cdot 0.4 + 0.8\cdot 2 + (-0.3)\cdot 5 + (-2) = 1.2 + 1.6 - 1.5 - 2 = -0.7\\ a &= \sigma(-0.7) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{0.7}} \approx 0.3318 \end{aligned}$$

The probability of default is about 33%. With a threshold of $0.5$ the loan will be approved; with a stricter threshold of $0.3$ it will not: the threshold is chosen by the business, not by the formula. The signs of the weights read as rules: the debt load and late payments increase the risk, a long credit history reduces it. This is logistic regression; it is trained with binary cross-entropy.

The numbers in the cases are illustrative. In real credit scoring the features are standardised and the weights are checked for common sense: for example, more late payments must not lower the risk. The ability to read every weight is the main reason why banks still love logistic regression.

Case 3. Logic: a neuron as a gate ($f$ is the step)

The inputs are zeros and ones, and $f(z) = 1$ for $z \ge 0$. Let us choose the weights so that the neuron computes the logical AND: $\mathbf{w} = (1,\ 1)$, $b = -1.5$. The sum $x_1 + x_2$ reaches $1.5$ only when both inputs are ones. For OR it is enough to raise the bias to $b = -0.5$: a single one suffices. For NOT a single input is enough: $w = -1$, $b = 0.5$.

Exclusive OR (XOR: 1 when the inputs differ) cannot be solved by a single neuron. The points $(0,\ 1)$ and $(1,\ 0)$ must end up on one side of the line, and $(0,\ 0)$ and $(1,\ 1)$ on the other, but they are arranged “crosswise”, and no such line exists.

Proof that XOR is beyond a single neuron

Suppose $w_1$, $w_2$, $b$ exist. Write down the four requirements:

$$\begin{aligned} (0,0)\mapsto 0:&\quad b < 0\\ (1,0)\mapsto 1:&\quad w_1 + b \ge 0\\ (0,1)\mapsto 1:&\quad w_2 + b \ge 0\\ (1,1)\mapsto 0:&\quad w_1 + w_2 + b < 0 \end{aligned}$$

Add the second and the third: $w_1 + w_2 + 2b \ge 0$, that is, $w_1 + w_2 + b \ge -b$. From the first, $-b > 0$, so $w_1 + w_2 + b > 0$, which contradicts the fourth. No such weights exist.

XOR and the “AI winter”

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert, in the book Perceptrons, rigorously demonstrated the limitations of the single-layer perceptron, including with the XOR example. Interest in neural networks faded for a long time. The way out turned out to be simple: two layers, $h_1 = \mathrm{OR}(x_1, x_2)$, $h_2 = \mathrm{NAND}(x_1, x_2)$, $y = \mathrm{AND}(h_1, h_2)$. Each of the three neurons is linear, but together they solve a non-linear problem. Training such networks at scale became possible after backpropagation was popularised in 1986.

Case 4. Computer vision: an edge detector ($f$ is ReLU)

The inputs are the nine brightnesses of a 3×3 image patch (0 is black, 1 is white), so $n = 9$. It is convenient to lay the weights out on the same grid:

$$\begin{pmatrix} w_1 & w_2 & w_3\\ w_4 & w_5 & w_6\\ w_7 & w_8 & w_9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1\\ -1 & 0 & 1\\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b = -1$$

The neuron adds up the brightnesses of the right column and subtracts the left one. On a “dark on the left, light on the right” patch (the left column is zeros, the middle and right ones are ones), $z = 3 - 0 - 1 = 2$ and $a = \mathrm{ReLU}(2) = 2$: the neuron fires. On a uniform white patch, $z = 3 - 3 - 1 = -1$ and $a = 0$: the neuron stays silent. The bias $-1$ is the sensitivity threshold: the neuron ignores weak changes in brightness.

A convolutional network is the same neuron sliding over the whole image with the same weights and building a map of “where an edge was found”. There is only one difference from our example: the network fits the filter weights itself, from the data.

From one neuron to a layer

A layer is several neurons that look at the same input $\mathbf{x}$, but each has its own weights and its own bias. Neuron number $j$ computes $a_j = f(\mathbf{w}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b_j)$. Stack the weight vectors as rows into a matrix $W$ of size $m\times n$, and the biases into a vector $\mathbf{b}$:

$$\mathbf{a} = f\big(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\big), \qquad W = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{w}_1^{\top}\\ \vdots\\ \mathbf{w}_m^{\top} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} b_1\\ \vdots\\ b_m \end{pmatrix}$$

The function $f$ is applied to each coordinate separately. An example: our neuron and a second one with weights $\mathbf{w}_2 = (-1,\ 0.5,\ 1)$ and bias $b_2 = 0.5$, with ReLU activation:

$$W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & -1 & 0.25\\ -1 & 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2\\ -1\\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} -0.75\\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.75\\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} -0.75\\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

After ReLU, $\mathbf{a} = (2,\ 1)$. Each row of $W$ is a separate neuron, each coordinate of $\mathbf{a}$ is its output. The layer has $m(n + 1) = 2\cdot 4 = 8$ parameters.

A batch of objects

In practice a network processes a batch of $N$ objects at once. Stack them as rows into a matrix $X$ of size $N\times n$:

$$\underbrace{Z}_{N\times m} = \underbrace{X}_{N\times n}\,\underbrace{W^{\top}}_{n\times m} + \mathbf{1}\mathbf{b}^{\top}, \qquad A = f(Z)$$

The term $\mathbf{1}\mathbf{b}^{\top}$ simply adds $\mathbf{b}^{\top}$ to every row (in NumPy and PyTorch, broadcasting does this). This is exactly the formula that nn.Linear computes in PyTorch: $y = xA^{\top} + b$.

The neuron inside a network: a 3D model

So far the neuron has stood alone. Let us see where it sits in a real, if tiny, neural network. Take the 3–3–1 network: three inputs, a hidden layer of three neurons and one output neuron. Our neuron from section 3, with weights $\mathbf{w} = (0.5,\ -1,\ 0.25)$ and bias $b = -0.75$, becomes the first neuron of the hidden layer, $h_1$. The input is the same, $\mathbf{x} = (2,\ -1,\ 3)$, and the activation is the sigmoid everywhere.

What the model shows

  • Every circle in the hidden and output layers is the same neuron with the formula $a = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b)$. Only the numbers differ: the weights and the bias.
  • Inputs are not neurons. They are just numbers from the data: they compute nothing and hand their values to every neuron of the next layer.
  • Lines are weights. Thickness is the absolute value of the weight, colour is its sign. Every hidden neuron has three incoming lines (its $w_1, w_2, w_3$); the output neuron has three lines from the hidden neurons.
  • The output of one neuron is the input of another. The number $0.8808$ that our neuron computes does not “leave” the network: it becomes the input $h_1$ of the output neuron, with weight $1.5$.
  • A layer is a row of a matrix. The weights of the three hidden neurons are the three rows of the matrix $W^{(1)}$; our neuron is its first row (section 9).

The forward pass in numbers

$$\mathbf{h} = \sigma\big(W^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}\big) = \sigma\!\left(\begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & -1 & 0.25\\ -1 & 0.5 & 1\\ 0.3 & 0.8 & -0.5 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2\\ -1\\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} -0.75\\ 0.5\\ 0.2 \end{pmatrix}\right) = \sigma\!\begin{pmatrix} 2\\ 1\\ -1.5 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.8808\\ 0.7311\\ 0.1824 \end{pmatrix}$$
$$\hat y = \sigma\big(\mathbf{w}^{(2)\top}\mathbf{h} + b^{(2)}\big) = \sigma(1.5\cdot 0.8808 - 1\cdot 0.7311 + 2\cdot 0.1824 - 0.5) = \sigma(0.455) \approx 0.6118$$

The first row of the first formula is exactly our calculation from section 3: $z_1 = 2$, $h_1 = \sigma(2) \approx 0.8808$. The whole network is four neurons, each computing one and the same formula.

The backward pass: how the error reaches our neuron

Let the correct answer be $y = 1$. The network answered $\hat y \approx 0.6118$, the loss is $L = -\ln \hat y \approx 0.4913$, and the error of the output neuron is $\delta_{\hat y} = \hat y - y \approx -0.3882$ (section 11). Our neuron never sees the answer $y$ directly: its error comes from the output neuron along the same weight $1.5$ its signal travelled along, multiplied by the derivative of its sigmoid:

$$\delta_{h_1} = \sigma'(z_1)\cdot w^{(2)}_1\cdot\delta_{\hat y} \approx 0.1050\cdot 1.5\cdot(-0.3882) \approx -0.0611, \qquad \sigma'(2) = 0.8808\cdot 0.1192 \approx 0.1050$$

From here it is exactly the single-neuron formula of section 11: the gradient with respect to its weights is the error times its inputs:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_1}L = \delta_{h_1}\,\mathbf{x} \approx -0.0611\cdot(2,\ -1,\ 3) \approx (-0.1223,\ 0.0611,\ -0.1834)$$

The minus sign of $\delta_{h_1}$ reads like this: to bring $\hat y$ closer to 1, our neuron should become even more active, because its output enters the answer with the positive weight $1.5$. A descent step will increase $w_1$ and $w_3$ (their inputs are positive) and decrease $w_2$ (its input is negative).

The point

The formula of a single neuron is shared by every circle of the network. The network adds only two things: the output of one neuron becomes the input of another (the forward pass), and the error comes back along the same weights in the opposite direction (backpropagation). Networks with billions of parameters work the same way, with more circles and more layers.

How a neuron learns

Where do the weights come from? They are fitted to the data. You define a loss function $L$, a number that shows how far the output $a$ is from the correct answer $y$, and shift each parameter against its gradient many times:

$$w_i \leftarrow w_i - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}, \qquad b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}$$

Here $\eta$ is the learning rate. The neuron’s formula makes the derivatives remarkably simple. By the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\cdot\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_i} = \delta\cdot x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \delta, \qquad \delta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\,f'(z)$$

The key is the derivatives of the weighted sum: $z = w_1x_1 + \dots + w_nx_n + b$ is linear in each weight, so $\partial z/\partial w_i = x_i$ and $\partial z/\partial b = 1$. The number $\delta$ is the “neuron’s error”, shared by all its weights. The gradient with respect to a weight equals the error multiplied by the input. If the input was zero, the weight does not change: it took no part in this answer.

In vector form the whole gradient is written in one line: $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}L = \delta\,\mathbf{x}$. For a sigmoid with binary cross-entropy the neuron’s error simplifies to the difference between the answer and the target: $\delta = a - y$.

Derivation: $\delta = a - y$ for the sigmoid and BCE
$$L = -\big[y\ln a + (1-y)\ln(1-a)\big], \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = -\frac{y}{a} + \frac{1-y}{1-a} = \frac{a-y}{a(1-a)}$$

The derivative of the sigmoid is expressed through the sigmoid itself: $\sigma'(z) = a(1-a)$. Multiply:

$$\delta = \frac{a-y}{a(1-a)}\cdot a(1-a) = a - y$$

The factors $a(1-a)$ cancel, which is why the “sigmoid + BCE” pair does not suffer from saturation at the output: the gradient is simply proportional to the size of the error.

One training step with our numbers

Let the correct answer be $y = 0$, while the neuron output $a \approx 0.8808$, a large error. The neuron’s error is $\delta = a - y \approx 0.8808$, and the learning rate is $\eta = 0.1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}L &= \delta\,\mathbf{x} \approx 0.8808\cdot(2,\ -1,\ 3) = (1.7616,\ -0.8808,\ 2.6424)\\ \mathbf{w} &\leftarrow (0.5,\ -1,\ 0.25) - 0.1\cdot(1.7616,\ -0.8808,\ 2.6424) \approx (0.3238,\ -0.9119,\ -0.0142)\\ b &\leftarrow -0.75 - 0.1\cdot 0.8808 \approx -0.8381 \end{aligned}$$

With the new parameters $z \approx 0.6788$ and $a \approx 0.6635$, and the loss fell from $2.1269$ to $1.0891$ in a single step. Each weight moved in proportion to its input: $w_3$ changed the most (the input $x_3 = 3$ is the largest), while $w_2$ even grew, because the input $x_2$ is negative.

Initialisation

A single neuron can be trained starting from zero weights; that is exactly what is done in logistic regression. But in a layer of many neurons identical initial weights are a trap: all neurons receive identical gradients and remain copies of each other forever. That is why layer weights are initialised randomly (Xavier and He initialisation).

One formula, three levels of code. The loop mirrors the per-coordinate notation symbol for symbol, NumPy mirrors the vector one, and PyTorch adds automatic differentiation.

Per coordinate: a loop, just like the formula

pythonimport math

def neuron(x, w, b, f):
    z = b
    for i in range(len(x)):       # Σ over i: 1 to n in the formula, 0 to n − 1 in Python
        z += w[i] * x[i]          # contribution of the i-th input
    return f(z)

sigmoid = lambda z: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))
print(neuron([2, -1, 3], [0.5, -1, 0.25], -0.75, sigmoid))   # 0.8807970779778823

Example 5matricescalculation

Four inputs: the sum, the vector and one common mistake

The goal

Check for $n = 4$ that the per-coordinate and the vector notations give the same number, and see what happens when the transposition is put in the wrong place.

$$\mathbf{x} = (1,\ 0,\ -2,\ 4), \qquad \mathbf{w} = (3,\ 5,\ 0.5,\ -1), \qquad b = 2$$

Per coordinate:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{4} w_ix_i &= 3\cdot1 + 5\cdot0 + 0.5\cdot(-2) + (-1)\cdot4 = 3 + 0 - 1 - 4 = -2\\ z &= -2 + 2 = 0 \end{aligned}$$

In vector form, row times column, with the same four products:

$$\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 5 & 0.5 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-2\\4\end{pmatrix} = -2 = \mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{w}$$

$z = 0$, so $a = \sigma(0) = 0.5$: the object lies exactly on the decision boundary. Now the mistake: move the transposition. $\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}^{\top}$ is a $4\times1$ column times a $1\times4$ row, that is, the $4\times4$ matrix of all pairwise products $w_ix_j$:

$$\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{3} & 0 & -6 & 12\\ 5 & \mathbf{0} & -10 & 20\\ 0.5 & 0 & \mathbf{-1} & 2\\ -1 & 0 & 2 & \mathbf{-4} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}^{\top}) = 3 + 0 - 1 - 4 = -2$$
What the example shows

The dot product is the sum of the diagonal (the trace) of the outer product: the neuron needs only the pairs with the same index, $w_ix_i$. If your code produces an $n\times n$ matrix instead of a number, the transposition is in the wrong place.

Example 17code

Everything together: a logistic neuron from scratch in NumPy

The goal

Assemble everything from the article in a couple of dozen lines: the vector form $z = X\mathbf{w} + b$, the sigmoid, BCE, the gradient $\delta\,\mathbf{x}$ and the descent step, and make sure that it works.

pythonimport numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 200
X = np.vstack([rng.normal([-1, -1], 1.0, size=(N // 2, 2)),    # class 0
               rng.normal([+1, +1], 1.0, size=(N // 2, 2))])   # class 1
y = np.r_[np.zeros(N // 2), np.ones(N // 2)]

w = np.zeros(2)
b = 0.0
eta = 0.5

for epoch in range(301):
    z = X @ w + b                      # (N,)  wᵀx + b for every object at once
    a = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))           # sigmoid
    loss = -np.mean(y * np.log(a) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - a))
    delta = a - y                      # δ = a − y
    w -= eta * X.T @ delta / N         # ∂L/∂w = mean of δ·x
    b -= eta * delta.mean()            # ∂L/∂b = mean of δ
    if epoch % 100 == 0:
        acc = np.mean((z >= 0) == y)
        print(f"epoch {epoch:3d}  loss {loss:.4f}  acc {acc:.2f}")

print("w =", w.round(3), " b =", round(b, 3))

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up dimensions. $\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}^{\top}$ instead of $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ gives an $n\times n$ matrix instead of a number. In code, check the shapes (.shape): $(N, n)$ times $(n, m)$ gives $(N, m)$.
  • Forgetting the bias. The boundary is nailed to the origin, and the model cannot describe even a simple shift.
  • Not scaling the inputs. A feature with large values dominates, and gradient descent zigzags and converges slowly.
  • A sigmoid in every hidden layer of a deep network. Its derivative is at most $0.25$, and the gradient vanishes from layer to layer. Hidden layers use ReLU and its relatives.
  • Identical initial weights in a layer. The neurons remain clones of each other.
  • Expecting a non-linear boundary from a single neuron. XOR, “a circle inside a ring”: you need a hidden layer or new features ($x_1x_2$, $x_1^2$).
  • Getting the indices wrong. In the formula $i$ runs from 1 to $n$, in Python from 0 to $n - 1$.
  • Computing the sigmoid and the log of the loss separately. For large $|z|$ you end up with $\log 0$. Use numerically stable functions that take logits, for example BCEWithLogitsLoss.

Summary

  • A neuron computes $a = f\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_ix_i + b\big) = f(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b)$: weigh, add up, shift, pass through $f$.
  • $x_i$ are data, $w_i$ and $b$ are trainable parameters (there are $n + 1$ of them), $z$ is the weighted sum, $f$ is the architect’s choice, $a$ is the answer.
  • The sum $\sum w_ix_i$ and the dot product $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ are one and the same; geometrically it is $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\cos\theta$, a measure of how similar the input is to the weight template.
  • The neuron’s boundary $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ is a hyperplane: $\mathbf{w}$ sets its orientation and sharpness, $b$ its position. A single neuron is a linear classifier.
  • The choice of $f$ determines the profession: identity means regression, sigmoid means probability, step means logic, ReLU means a feature detector.
  • A layer is a weight matrix: $\mathbf{a} = f(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$; a batch is $A = f(XW^{\top} + \mathbf{1}\mathbf{b}^{\top})$.
  • Learning rests on a simple derivative: $\partial L/\partial w_i = \delta\,x_i$, $\partial L/\partial b = \delta$.
  • In a network every circle is the same neuron: the output of one becomes the input of another, and the error comes back along the same weights. The 3–3–1 network is 4 neurons and 16 parameters.

Test yourself

1. A neuron with the identity activation: $\mathbf{x} = (1,\ 2)$, $\mathbf{w} = (3,\ -1)$, $b = 0.5$. What is the output?
$z = 3\cdot1 + (-1)\cdot2 + 0.5 = 1.5$, and $a = z$.
2. How many trainable parameters does a neuron with 10 inputs have?
10 weights and one bias: $n + 1 = 11$.
3. What is the size of $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}$ if $\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{x}$ are columns of length $n$?
$(1\times n)(n\times 1) = 1\times 1$. An $n\times n$ matrix would come from $\mathbf{w}\mathbf{x}^{\top}$.
4. What happens to the decision boundary if you increase only the bias $b$?
The normal $\mathbf{w}$ does not change, so the slope stays the same; what changes is the distance $|b|/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ to the origin.
5. A neuron $\sigma(wx + b)$ with $w = 2$, $b = -6$. For which $x$ is the output equal to $0.5$?
$\sigma(z) = 0.5$ at $z = 0$: $2x - 6 = 0$, $x_0 = -b/w = 3$.
6. Why can a single neuron not compute XOR?
With a monotonic $f$ the boundary is the line $w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b = 0$, while the XOR points are arranged “crosswise”; a second layer is needed.
7. What is the derivative $\partial z/\partial w_i$?
$z$ is linear in $w_i$ with coefficient $x_i$. Hence $\partial L/\partial w_i = \delta\,x_i$.
8. How many trainable parameters does the 3–3–1 network have (three inputs, three hidden neurons, one output)?
Hidden layer: $3\cdot(3 + 1) = 12$, output: $1\cdot(3 + 1) = 4$, 16 in total.
