The chain rule: the derivative of a composite function from the definition to neural networks

Almost every function you have to differentiate is a function of a function: the sine of a square, the logarithm of a sum, the sigmoid of a weighted sum. The chain rule says how to find its derivative: multiply the derivatives of the links. We derive the rule from the definition of the derivative, prove it, extend it to functions of several variables, tell ordinary derivatives from partial ones, and solve dozens of examples — from the very simplest to the backward pass of a neuron.

Why we need the chain rule

Everyone knows the derivatives of simple functions: $(x^2)' = 2x$, $(\sin x)' = \cos x$, $(e^x)' = e^x$. But the functions you meet in practice are almost never simple. They are functions of functions: the sine not of $x$ but of $x^2$; the exponential not of $x$ but of $-x^2$; the logarithm not of $x$ but of $\cos x$.

$$\sin(x^2),\qquad e^{-x^2},\qquad \ln(\cos x),\qquad \sqrt{1 + e^{2x}},\qquad \sigma(wx + b) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(wx + b)}}$$

Such a function is built like a chain: first an intermediate quantity is computed from $x$, then the next one from it, and so on until the answer. The chain rule answers the question: how fast does the answer change if we change the input just a little? The answer is surprisingly simple: multiply the rates of change of all the links of the chain:

$$\big(f(g(x))\big)' = f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x)$$

For functions of several variables the rule sounds the same, except that several links can run in parallel, and then the contributions of all the paths add up:

$$z = f\big(x(t),\ y(t)\big)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dt}$$
An analogy

Three gears. The first one turns the second so that the second makes 3 revolutions for every revolution of the first; the second turns the third, 2 revolutions per revolution of its own. How many revolutions does the third make per revolution of the first? $3 \cdot 2 = 6$. Gear ratios multiply. The derivative is exactly the “gear ratio” of a function at a given point, and the chain rule is the rule of gears.

In this article we will go all the way: recall the definition of the derivative as a limit and learn to compute with it; work out what a composite function is; derive and prove the chain rule straight from the definition; extend it to partial derivatives and functions of two or more variables; understand how an ordinary derivative differs from a partial one and from a total one; and see that training a neural network (the backward pass) is the chain rule applied many times over. At the end there are 10 detailed walkthroughs and 56 examples solved step by step, from very easy to hard.

The main point

The chain rule is not a separate trick but a consequence of the very definition of the derivative: the derivative shows how many times a function stretches a small step of its argument. If the step passes through several functions in a row, the stretches multiply.

The derivative from the definition

Let us start with the foundation. Let $y = f(x)$ be a function of one variable. Give the argument a small increment $h$ (also written $\Delta x$): move from the point $x$ to the point $x + h$. The function changes by $\Delta f = f(x + h) - f(x)$. The ratio of these changes is the difference quotient:

$$\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$$

It shows the average rate of change of the function on the interval $[x,\ x + h]$, and geometrically, the slope of the secant through the points of the graph $(x,\ f(x))$ and $(x + h,\ f(x + h))$. If we let $h$ tend to zero, the secant rotates and turns into the tangent, and the average rate turns into the instantaneous one. This limit is called the derivative:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$$
$f'(x)$
the derivative of the function $f$ at the point $x$ (Lagrange’s notation); the same thing is written $\frac{dy}{dx}$ or $\frac{df}{dx}$ (Leibniz’s notation)
$h$
the increment of the argument, $h \ne 0$; also written $\Delta x$
$f(x + h) - f(x)$
the increment of the function, $\Delta f$ (or $\Delta y$)
$\lim_{h \to 0}$
the limit: the value that the difference quotient gets arbitrarily close to when $h$ becomes arbitrarily small (but not equal to zero)

How to compute a derivative from the definition: the algorithm

  1. Write down $f(x + h)$: substitute $x + h$ for every $x$.
  2. Find the difference $f(x + h) - f(x)$ and simplify it so that a factor of $h$ appears (expand the brackets, bring to a common denominator, multiply by the conjugate, apply a trigonometric identity).
  3. Divide by $h$ and cancel. This is legitimate because $h \ne 0$: the value $h = 0$ is not considered in the definition of the limit.
  4. Pass to the limit $h \to 0$: substitute $h = 0$ into what remains (or use a known limit).

Example 1: $f(x) = x^2$. Step 1: $f(x + h) = (x + h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2$. Step 2: $f(x + h) - f(x) = 2xh + h^2 = h(2x + h)$. Step 3: $\frac{h(2x + h)}{h} = 2x + h$. Step 4: $\lim_{h \to 0}(2x + h) = 2x$. So $(x^2)' = 2x$.

Example 2: $f(x) = \sin x$. By the difference-of-sines formula, $\sin(x + h) - \sin x = 2\cos\!\big(x + \tfrac{h}{2}\big)\sin\tfrac{h}{2}$. Divide by $h$:

$$\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \cos\!\Big(x + \frac{h}{2}\Big)\cdot\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \;\xrightarrow[h \to 0]{}\; \cos x \cdot 1 = \cos x$$

Here we used the first fundamental limit $\lim_{t \to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1$.

Example 3: $f(x) = e^x$. $\frac{e^{x + h} - e^x}{h} = e^x\cdot\frac{e^h - 1}{h} \to e^x\cdot 1 = e^x$, by the second fundamental limit in the form $\lim_{t \to 0}\frac{e^t - 1}{t} = 1$ (in essence, this is the definition of the number $e$).

The linear approximation is the key to the proof

The definition of the derivative can be rewritten without a fraction. If $f'(x)$ exists, then for small $h$
$f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)\,h + \varepsilon(h)\,h$, where $\varepsilon(h) \to 0$ as $h \to 0$.
That is, near the point the function behaves like a straight line with slope $f'(x)$, and the error $\varepsilon(h)\,h$ is small even compared with $h$. This is exactly the form we will use to prove the chain rule.

Why may we cancel $h$ if in the end $h \to 0$?

The limit as $h \to 0$ describes the behaviour of an expression for $h$ close to zero but not equal to it. So throughout the computation $h \ne 0$, and dividing by $h$ is legitimate. For example, the functions $\frac{h(2x + h)}{h}$ and $2x + h$ coincide for all $h \ne 0$, so their limits as $h \to 0$ are equal. The fraction itself is undefined at $h = 0$, but the limit does not ask about that value.

Composite functions: outer and inner

A composite function (a composition) is a function whose argument is itself a function. If $u = g(x)$ and $y = f(u)$, then

$$y = f\big(g(x)\big) = (f \circ g)(x)$$

Here $g$ is the inner function (it is computed first), $f$ is the outer function (it is computed last), and $u$ is the intermediate variable. To find the outer function, ask yourself: “Which operation would I perform last if I were computing the value on a calculator?” Everything under that operation is the inner function.

Order matters

$\sin(x^2)$ and $\sin^2 x$ are different functions. In the first, you square first and then take the sine; in the second, the other way round. At $x = 2$: $\sin 4 \approx -0.757$, while $\sin^2 2 \approx 0.827$. Composition is not commutative: $f \circ g \ne g \circ f$.

The chain can be longer: $y = \sin^2(3x)$ consists of three links $v = 3x$, $u = \sin v$, $y = u^2$. It is convenient to write such a function as a pipeline:

$$x \xrightarrow{\ \cdot 3\ } v = 3x \xrightarrow{\ \sin\ } u = \sin v \xrightarrow{\ (\cdot)^2\ } y = u^2$$

This is exactly how a neural network is built: the input passes through layer after layer (see the article “The forward pass formula”), and every layer is another link of the chain.

The chain rule for a function of one variable

Theorem (the chain rule). Let the function $g$ be differentiable at the point $x$, and the function $f$ be differentiable at the point $u = g(x)$. Then the composite function $y = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at the point $x$, and

$$\big(f(g(x))\big)' = f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x)$$

In Leibniz’s notation, with $u = g(x)$, the rule looks like “cancelling fractions”:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$$
$f'\big(g(x)\big)$
the derivative of the outer function, evaluated at the point $u = g(x)$, not at the point $x$
$g'(x)$
the derivative of the inner function at the point $x$
$\frac{dy}{du}$
the rate of change of $y$ with respect to the intermediate variable $u$
$\frac{du}{dx}$
the rate of change of $u$ with respect to $x$

In words: the derivative of the outer function (with the inner one left untouched inside) times the derivative of the inner function. For a long chain $y = f_1(f_2(\cdots f_n(x)))$ the derivatives of all the links multiply:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du_1}\cdot\frac{du_1}{du_2}\cdots\frac{du_{n-1}}{dx}$$

How to apply it: the algorithm

  1. Split into layers. Find the outer function $f(u)$ and the inner one $u = g(x)$ (the last operation is the outer one).
  2. Differentiate the outer function with respect to its argument $u$, as if $u$ were an ordinary variable: $\frac{dy}{du} = f'(u)$.
  3. Differentiate the inner function with respect to $x$: $\frac{du}{dx} = g'(x)$. If the inner function is itself composite, repeat the algorithm for it.
  4. Multiply and substitute $u = g(x)$ back.

Example. $y = \sin(x^2)$. Layers: $y = \sin u$, $u = x^2$. Outer: $\frac{dy}{du} = \cos u$. Inner: $\frac{du}{dx} = 2x$. Product: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \cos u\cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2)$.

Why the derivatives multiply

The derivative is the stretch factor of small steps. The inner function stretches the step $\Delta x$ by a factor of $g'(x)$: $\Delta u \approx g'(x)\,\Delta x$. The outer function stretches that step by a factor of $f'(u)$: $\Delta y \approx f'(u)\,\Delta u \approx f'(u)\,g'(x)\,\Delta x$. Two stretches in a row amount to their product.

The proof from the definition

Let us prove the chain rule relying only on the definition of the derivative. First we will look at the “almost proof” that comes to mind first and see where it breaks down.

A naive attempt

Let $u = g(x)$, $\Delta u = g(x + h) - g(x)$, $\Delta y = f(u + \Delta u) - f(u)$. Then it is tempting to write

$$\frac{\Delta y}{h} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{h} \;\xrightarrow[h \to 0]{}\; f'(u)\cdot g'(x)$$

The idea is right: as $h \to 0$ the increment $\Delta u \to 0$ (a differentiable function is continuous), so the first fraction tends to $f'(u)$ and the second to $g'(x)$. But there is a hole: $\Delta u$ can vanish for $h \ne 0$, and then the fraction $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}$ is undefined. This happens not only in exotic cases: for example, for $g(x) = \text{const}$ the increment $\Delta u = 0$ for every $h$; for functions like $g(x) = \sin\frac{1}{x}$, for infinitely many $h$ near zero. We need a proof that does not divide by $\Delta u$.

A rigorous proof

Step 1. Write the differentiability of $f$ without division. Introduce the auxiliary function

$$\varphi(k) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{f(u + k) - f(u)}{k} - f'(u), & k \ne 0,\\[2mm] 0, & k = 0. \end{cases}$$

By the definition of the derivative $f'(u)$ we have $\varphi(k) \to 0$ as $k \to 0$, and we set $\varphi(0) = 0$ ourselves, so $\varphi$ is continuous at zero. From the definition of $\varphi$ we obtain an equality that holds for all $k$, including $k = 0$:

$$f(u + k) - f(u) = \big(f'(u) + \varphi(k)\big)\cdot k$$

(For $k = 0$ both sides are zero; for $k \ne 0$ this is just the definition of $\varphi$ rewritten.)

Step 2. Substitute $k = \Delta u$. Take $k = \Delta u = g(x + h) - g(x)$. Then $u + k = g(x + h)$ and

$$f\big(g(x + h)\big) - f\big(g(x)\big) = \big(f'(u) + \varphi(\Delta u)\big)\cdot\Delta u$$

Step 3. Divide by $h \ne 0$; there is no longer any need to divide by $\Delta u$:

$$\frac{f\big(g(x + h)\big) - f\big(g(x)\big)}{h} = \big(f'(u) + \varphi(\Delta u)\big)\cdot\frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h}$$

Step 4. Pass to the limit $h \to 0$. By definition, the second fraction tends to $g'(x)$. The function $g$ is differentiable and hence continuous at $x$: $\Delta u \to 0$. Since $\varphi$ is continuous at zero, $\varphi(\Delta u) \to \varphi(0) = 0$. The limit of a product equals the product of the limits:

$$\begin{aligned}\big(f(g(x))\big)' &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f\big(g(x + h)\big) - f\big(g(x)\big)}{h}\\ &= \big(f'(u) + 0\big)\cdot g'(x) = f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x)\qquad\blacksquare\end{aligned}$$
What the proof has shown

The chain rule is not a new axiom but a direct consequence of the definition of the derivative. All the work comes down to one observation: the increment of a composite function equals “the slope of the outer function” × “the increment of the inner one”, and the increment of the inner one ≈ “the slope of the inner function” × $h$.

The same derivation via linear approximations

Write both functions as “a straight line + a small error”: $g(x + h) = g(x) + g'(x)h + o(h)$ and $f(u + k) = f(u) + f'(u)k + o(k)$. Substitute $k = g'(x)h + o(h)$:

$$\begin{aligned}f\big(g(x + h)\big) &= f(u) + f'(u)\big(g'(x)h + o(h)\big) + o(k)\\ &= f(u) + f'(u)\,g'(x)\,h + o(h)\end{aligned}$$

(Here $o(k) = o(h)$ because $k$ is at most a constant times $h$.) The coefficient of $h$ is the derivative: $f'(u)\,g'(x)$. This point of view, “the derivative = the best linear approximation”, generalizes directly to many variables: there, numbers are replaced by matrices, and the product of numbers by the product of matrices.

The chain rule and the definition: computing it two ways

The best way to believe in the chain rule is to compute the same derivative from the definition and by the rule and to see that the very same product appears inside the limit “all by itself”.

$f(x) = (3x + 1)^2$

  1. From the definition. Write down the difference quotient.

    $$\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{(3x + 3h + 1)^2 - (3x + 1)^2}{h}$$

  2. Let $u = 3x + 1$; then $3x + 3h + 1 = u + 3h$. Expand the difference of squares: $(u + 3h)^2 - u^2 = 2u\cdot 3h + 9h^2$.

    $$\frac{(u + 3h)^2 - u^2}{h} = \frac{6uh + 9h^2}{h} = 6u + 9h$$

  3. Pass to the limit $h \to 0$.

    $$f'(x) = 6u = 6(3x + 1) = 18x + 6$$

  4. By the chain rule. $y = u^2$, $u = 3x + 1$.

    $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \underbrace{2u}_{dy/du}\cdot\underbrace{3}_{du/dx} = 6(3x + 1)$$

  5. What matched. Look at step 2: $6u = 2u\cdot 3$; the limit already “contains” the derivative of the outer function $2u$ and the derivative of the inner one $3$. The increment of the inner function $\Delta u = 3h$ is exactly $3$ times larger than $h$, and the outer function stretches it by another factor of $2u$.

$f(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 1}$

  1. From the definition. The roots prevent us from cancelling $h$, so we multiply by the conjugate expression $\sqrt{(x + h)^2 + 1} + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}$.

    $$\frac{\sqrt{(x + h)^2 + 1} - \sqrt{x^2 + 1}}{h} = \frac{\big((x + h)^2 + 1\big) - \big(x^2 + 1\big)}{h\left(\sqrt{(x + h)^2 + 1} + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}\right)}$$

  2. In the numerator, $(x + h)^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2 = h(2x + h)$; cancel $h$.

    $$= \frac{2x + h}{\sqrt{(x + h)^2 + 1} + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}}$$

  3. Pass to the limit $h \to 0$: the numerator $\to 2x$, the denominator $\to 2\sqrt{x^2 + 1}$.

    $$f'(x) = \frac{2x}{2\sqrt{x^2 + 1}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}$$

  4. By the chain rule. $y = \sqrt{u}$, $u = x^2 + 1$.

    $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\cdot 2x = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}$$

  5. What matched. In the limit, the numerator $2x$ is the derivative of the inner function $x^2 + 1$, and the denominator $2\sqrt{x^2 + 1}$ comes from the derivative of the outer one, $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}$.

Long chains and combinations with other rules

If there are more than two links, the rule is applied to each of them: the derivatives of all the links multiply, and each one is evaluated at its own point, at the value of its own argument.

Example: $y = \sin^2(3x)$. The pipeline: $v = 3x$, $u = \sin v$, $y = u^2$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dv}\cdot\frac{dv}{dx} = 2u\cdot\cos v\cdot 3 = 6\sin 3x\cos 3x = 3\sin 6x$$

Together with the product and quotient rules

The chain rule combines freely with the others: $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$, $\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$. Each factor is differentiated by the chain rule if it is composite itself:

$$\big(x^2 e^{3x}\big)' = 2x\cdot e^{3x} + x^2\cdot\underbrace{3e^{3x}}_{\text{chain rule}} = x\,e^{3x}(3x + 2)$$

The chain rule also explains the differentiation rules themselves. For example, the quotient rule follows from $\frac{u}{v} = u\cdot v^{-1}$: $(v^{-1})' = -v^{-2}\cdot v'$ is the chain rule with the outer function $t^{-1}$.

Logarithmic differentiation

For functions of the form $y = u(x)^{v(x)}$ (the variable is both in the base and in the exponent) we take the logarithm: $\ln y = v\ln u$. Differentiate both sides, remembering that $y = y(x)$; by the chain rule $(\ln y)' = \frac{y'}{y}$:

$$\frac{y'}{y} = v'\ln u + v\,\frac{u'}{u}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = u^{v}\Big(v'\ln u + \frac{v\,u'}{u}\Big)$$

For example, $(x^x)' = x^x(\ln x + 1)$.

Partial derivatives from the definition

Now for functions of several variables: $z = f(x, y)$, for example, the area of a rectangle $xy$, the distance to the origin $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, or the loss function of a neural network, which depends on thousands of weights. How do we measure its rate of change? We will change one variable at a time and freeze the rest.

The partial derivative with respect to $x$ is the ordinary derivative with respect to $x$ with $y$ held fixed:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x + h,\ y) - f(x,\ y)}{h}$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x,\ y + h) - f(x,\ y)}{h}$$
$\partial$
the partial derivative sign (“curly d”, or “partial”): it emphasizes that the function has other variables too, which do not change at that moment
$f_x,\ f'_x,\ \partial_x f$
other notations for the same partial derivative with respect to $x$
$\nabla f = (f_x,\ f_y)$
the gradient: the vector of all the partial derivatives; it points in the direction of the fastest growth of the function

Geometrically, fixing $y = y_0$ means cutting the surface $z = f(x, y)$ with the vertical plane $y = y_0$. The cross-section is a curve, the graph of the one-variable function $x \mapsto f(x, y_0)$, and $f_x$ is the slope of the tangent to this curve.

An example from the definition: $f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin y$

  1. With respect to $x$. Only $x$ gets an increment; $y$ is a number.

    $$\frac{f(x + h, y) - f(x, y)}{h} = \frac{(x + h)^2 y + \sin y - x^2 y - \sin y}{h}$$

  2. The term $\sin y$ does not depend on $x$ and cancels; expand $(x + h)^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2$.

    $$= \frac{(2xh + h^2)\,y}{h} = (2x + h)\,y \;\xrightarrow[h \to 0]{}\; 2xy$$

  3. With respect to $y$. Now only $y$ gets an increment; $x$ is a number.

    $$\frac{x^2(y + h) + \sin(y + h) - x^2 y - \sin y}{h} = x^2 + \frac{\sin(y + h) - \sin y}{h}$$

  4. The second fraction is the difference quotient of the sine; its limit is $\cos y$ (we derived it in the section on the definition).

    $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + \cos y$$

We see that by definition a partial derivative is an ordinary derivative in which the “other” variables are constants. Therefore all the rules, the chain rule included, work for it unchanged, as long as there is only one path from the function to the variable:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\sin(xy) = \cos(xy)\cdot\frac{\partial(xy)}{\partial x} = y\cos(xy),\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\sin(xy) = x\cos(xy)$$
A partial derivative is not the whole story

Partial derivatives describe how a function changes only along the axes. If $x$ and $y$ change simultaneously (for example, both depend on time), both changes must be taken into account at once, and this is where the multivariable chain rule begins.

The chain rule for two or more variables

Case 1: $z = f(x, y)$, where $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$

The point $(x(t), y(t))$ moves in the plane, and we watch how the value of the function at this point changes: $z(t) = f(x(t), y(t))$. This is a function of the single variable $t$, and it has an ordinary derivative $\frac{dz}{dt}$. The rule:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dt}$$

The meaning: $t$ affects $z$ along two paths, through $x$ and through $y$. Along each path the derivatives multiply (as in a one-variable chain), and the contributions of the paths add up. In vector form, it is the dot product of the gradient and the velocity of the point:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t) = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x'(t)\\ y'(t) \end{pmatrix}$$

Derivation from the definition

  1. Give $t$ an increment $\Delta t$. Then $x$ and $y$ get the increments $\Delta x = x(t + \Delta t) - x(t)$ and $\Delta y = y(t + \Delta t) - y(t)$.

  2. If $f$ is differentiable at the point $(x, y)$, its increment is “a plane + a small error” (the two-dimensional analogue of the linear approximation), where $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 \to 0$ as $(\Delta x, \Delta y) \to (0, 0)$:

    $$\Delta z = f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y + \varepsilon_1\Delta x + \varepsilon_2\Delta y$$

  3. Divide by $\Delta t$:

    $$\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta t} = f_x\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} + f_y\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} + \varepsilon_1\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} + \varepsilon_2\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}$$

  4. As $\Delta t \to 0$: $\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \to x'(t)$, $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} \to y'(t)$ (the definition of the derivatives), and $\Delta x, \Delta y \to 0$, so $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 \to 0$, and the last two terms vanish.

    $$\frac{dz}{dt} = f_x\,x'(t) + f_y\,y'(t)\qquad\blacksquare$$
Why we need differentiability, and not merely the existence of partial derivatives

Take $f(x, y) = \frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2}$ for $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$ and $f(0, 0) = 0$. By definition $f_x(0, 0) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(h, 0) - 0}{h} = 0$ and $f_y(0, 0) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(0, h) - 0}{h} = 0$. Let the point move along the diagonal: $x = t$, $y = t$. The formula would give $\frac{dz}{dt} = 0\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 1 = 0$. But in fact $z(t) = \frac{t^3}{2t^2} = \frac{t}{2}$, and $\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac12$.

The reason: partial derivatives “see” the function only along the axes, and near zero this function is not approximated by a plane. A sufficient condition in practice: if the partial derivatives exist and are continuous near the point, the function is differentiable and the chain rule works. This holds for all elementary functions at the points where they are “well-behaved”.

Case 2: $z = f(u, v)$, where $u = u(s, t)$, $v = v(s, t)$

Now the intermediate variables themselves depend on two variables. The derivatives of $z$ with respect to $s$ and to $t$ are partial. For each of them the same “over all paths” rule works:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial s},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}$$

How to use the tree. To find $\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}$, find all the paths from $z$ down to the leaves $s$ (there are two in the figure: through $u$ and through $v$). Along each path, multiply the derivatives on the edges. Add up the products of all the paths. The backward pass of a neural network performs the same procedure, only there the tree has millions of edges.

The general case: $n$ intermediate variables and the Jacobian matrix

If $z = f(u_1, \dots, u_m)$ and each $u_j$ depends on $x_1, \dots, x_n$, then

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x_i} = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial z}{\partial u_j}\cdot\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i},\qquad i = 1, \dots, n$$

In matrix form this is a product of Jacobian matrices. For maps $\mathbf{g}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $\mathbf{f}: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^k$:

$$J_{\mathbf{f}\circ\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}) = J_{\mathbf{f}}\big(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\big)\cdot J_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad J_{\mathbf{g}} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_n}\\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ \frac{\partial u_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial u_m}{\partial x_n} \end{pmatrix}$$

The sizes match: $(k \times m)\cdot(m \times n) = k \times n$. The scalar chain rule $f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ is the $1 \times 1$ special case. The sum over $j$ is just the row-times-column rule: each term is one path through $u_j$.

The rule in one sentence

The derivative with respect to a variable = the sum, over all paths from the function to that variable, of the products of the derivatives along the path. One path means one product (one variable); several paths mean a sum of products (several variables).

The total derivative: when a variable acts both directly and through another one

Let $z = f(x, y)$, but let $y$ itself depend on $x$: $y = y(x)$. Then $z$ is ultimately a function of the single variable $x$, and it has an ordinary derivative $\frac{dz}{dx}$, which is called the total derivative. There are two paths from $z$ to $x$: the direct one and the one through $y$:

$$\frac{dz}{dx} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}}_{\text{direct path}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}}_{\text{path through } y}$$

Example. $z = xy$, $y = x^2$. The partial derivative is $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = y = x^2$ (we treat $y$ as a constant). The total one: $\frac{dz}{dx} = y + x\cdot 2x = 3x^2$. A check by substitution: $z = x\cdot x^2 = x^3$, $(x^3)' = 3x^2$. The partial derivative answered the question “what happens if we change $x$ while holding $y$”, and the total one, “what actually happens if $y$ obediently follows $x$”.

A typical trap

The notation $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ without saying what is held constant can be ambiguous. That is why physics and thermodynamics write $\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_y$, “at constant $y$”. In ML the same role is played by the question “which variables are the leaves of the computational graph”.

Implicit functions

If $y(x)$ is defined by an equation $F(x, y) = 0$ (for example, $x^2 + y^2 = 25$), differentiate it with respect to $x$ as a total derivative: $F_x + F_y\cdot y' = 0$, whence

$$y' = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}\qquad (F_y \ne 0)$$

For the circle: $2x + 2y\,y' = 0$, $y' = -\frac{x}{y}$; at the point $(3,\ 4)$ the slope of the tangent is $-\frac34$. This is the chain rule too: $(y^2)' = 2y\cdot y'$, because $y$ is a function of $x$.

The chain rule in neural networks: the backward pass

To train a neural network means to find the weights at which the loss function $L$ is minimal. For that, gradient descent moves every weight against its partial derivative at each step: $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$. But $L$ depends on $w$ through a long chain: weight → weighted sum → activation → … → answer → loss. So $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ is found with the chain rule. The backpropagation algorithm is the chain rule applied from the end of the chain to its beginning, with the intermediate products stored.

Take a single neuron from the article “The formula of a single neuron” with a sigmoid and a squared-error loss function:

$$z = wx + b,\qquad a = \sigma(z),\qquad L = \tfrac12 (a - y)^2$$

  1. The forward pass. Let $x = 2$, $w = 0.5$, $b = -1$, and the correct answer $y = 1$.

    $$z = 0.5\cdot 2 - 1 = 0,\qquad a = \sigma(0) = 0.5,\qquad L = \tfrac12(0.5 - 1)^2 = 0.125$$

  2. The local derivatives of each link (for the sigmoid, $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$; see example No. 18 of the practice set).

    $$\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = a - y = -0.5,\qquad \frac{da}{dz} = a(1 - a) = 0.25,\\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = x = 2,\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial b} = 1\end{gathered}$$

  3. The backward pass: multiply from the end. The product $\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\cdot\frac{da}{dz}$ is shared by both parameters, so it is computed once (the neuron’s “error” $\delta$).

    $$\delta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = -0.5\cdot 0.25 = -0.125$$

  4. The gradients with respect to the parameters.

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \delta\cdot x = -0.25,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \delta\cdot 1 = -0.125$$

  5. A training step with $\eta = 1$: both parameters grow, $z$ increases, $a$ approaches $y = 1$, and the loss drops.

    $$\begin{gathered}w \leftarrow 0.5 + 0.25 = 0.75,\qquad b \leftarrow -1 + 0.125 = -0.875;\\ z = 0.625,\qquad a \approx 0.6514,\qquad L \approx 0.0608\end{gathered}$$

In a network of many layers (see the forward pass) every neuron affects the loss through all the neurons of the next layer: this is the multivariable chain rule, and the contributions of the paths add up. In matrix form, the error of a layer is computed from the error of the next one:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \Big(\big(W^{(l+1)}\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}\Big)\odot f'\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\big(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}\big)^{\top}$$

The transposed matrix $\big(W^{(l+1)}\big)^{\top}$ is the very Jacobian matrix of the linear layer, “turned around” for moving backward, and the sum hidden in the matrix–vector product is the sum over all paths.

Why backward and not forward

The derivative of a composite function can be multiplied out from either end. But a network has one output (the loss) and millions of inputs (the weights). If we go from the end, the common factor $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}}$ is computed once per layer and reused for all the weights. That is why the backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass, and this is what made training deep networks possible.

Example 1one variablefrom the definition

$y = (2x + 3)^2$ in three ways

The goal

Make sure that the chain rule gives the same result as expanding the brackets and as the definition of the derivative.

  1. Way 1: expand the brackets. $(2x + 3)^2 = 4x^2 + 12x + 9$; differentiate the polynomial term by term.

    $$y' = 8x + 12$$

  2. Way 2: from the definition. Let $u = 2x + 3$; when $x$ shifts by $h$, the inner function shifts by $2h$.

    $$\frac{(u + 2h)^2 - u^2}{h} = \frac{4uh + 4h^2}{h} = 4u + 4h \;\xrightarrow[h \to 0]{}\; 4u = 8x + 12$$

  3. Way 3: the chain rule. Outer $u^2$, inner $u = 2x + 3$.

    $$y' = 2u\cdot 2 = 4(2x + 3) = 8x + 12$$
What the example shows

All three ways give the same result. The factor $2$ from the inner function is not an “add-on”: it is how many times faster $u$ changes than $x$.

Example 2one variableorder of links

$\sin(x^2)$ versus $\sin^2 x$

The goal

See how both the function and its derivative depend on the order of the links.

  1. $y_1 = \sin(x^2)$: outer $\sin u$, inner $u = x^2$.

    $$y_1' = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x$$

  2. $y_2 = \sin^2 x$: outer $u^2$, inner $u = \sin x$.

    $$y_2' = 2\sin x\cdot\cos x = \sin 2x$$

  3. At the point $x = 1$ the values differ.

    $$y_1'(1) = 2\cos 1 \approx 1.0806,\qquad y_2'(1) = \sin 2 \approx 0.9093$$
What the example shows

Always determine first which operation comes last: that is the outer function.

Example 3one variablefrom the definition

$y = \sqrt{3x + 1}$: the definition and the rule

The goal

Find the derivative of a square root from the definition (via the conjugate) and see the chain rule in the answer.

  1. The difference quotient; multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate $\sqrt{3x + 3h + 1} + \sqrt{3x + 1}$.

    $$\frac{\sqrt{3x + 3h + 1} - \sqrt{3x + 1}}{h} = \frac{(3x + 3h + 1) - (3x + 1)}{h\big(\sqrt{3x + 3h + 1} + \sqrt{3x + 1}\big)}$$

  2. The numerator becomes $3h$; cancel $h$ and pass to the limit.

    $$= \frac{3}{\sqrt{3x + 3h + 1} + \sqrt{3x + 1}} \;\xrightarrow[h \to 0]{}\; \frac{3}{2\sqrt{3x + 1}}$$

  3. By the rule: $(\sqrt{u})' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}$, $u' = 3$.

    $$y' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3x + 1}}\cdot 3$$
What the example shows

The $3$ in the numerator is the derivative of the inner function; it appeared in the limit as $\Delta u = 3h$.

Example 5one variablelong chain

Three links: $y = \ln(\cos 3x)$

The goal

Apply the rule to a chain of three functions without losing a single factor.

  1. The pipeline: $v = 3x$, $u = \cos v$, $y = \ln u$.

Example 6two variablesfrom the definition

Partial derivatives of $f(x, y) = (x + y^2)^3$ from the definition

The goal

Find both partial derivatives from the definition and see that, with respect to $y$, the inner function $y^2$ contributes its own factor.

  1. Let $A = x + y^2$. With respect to $x$: only $x$ gets an increment, so $A$ shifts by $h$.

    $$\frac{(A + h)^3 - A^3}{h} = \frac{3A^2h + 3Ah^2 + h^3}{h} = 3A^2 + 3Ah + h^2 \;\to\; 3A^2$$

  2. With respect to $y$: $A$ shifts by $\Delta = (y + h)^2 - y^2 = 2yh + h^2$. We use $(A + \Delta)^3 - A^3 = \Delta\,(3A^2 + 3A\Delta + \Delta^2)$.

    $$\frac{\Delta\,(3A^2 + 3A\Delta + \Delta^2)}{h} = (2y + h)(3A^2 + 3A\Delta + \Delta^2) \;\to\; 2y\cdot 3A^2$$

  3. The result, checked with the chain rule: outer $A^3$, inner $A = x + y^2$, $A_x = 1$, $A_y = 2y$.

    $$f_x = 3(x + y^2)^2,\qquad f_y = 6y\,(x + y^2)^2$$
What the example shows

A partial derivative from the definition is the same procedure as for one variable; the chain rule works inside it unchanged.

Example 7two variablesgeometry

Motion along a circle: $z = x^2 + y^2$

The goal

Apply the rule $\frac{dz}{dt} = f_x x' + f_y y'$ and understand when the contributions of the paths cancel each other out.

  1. $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$. The partial derivatives and the velocities.

    $$f_x = 2x,\quad f_y = 2y,\qquad x' = -\sin t,\quad y' = \cos t$$

  2. The sum of the contributions of the two paths.

    $$\frac{dz}{dt} = 2\cos t\cdot(-\sin t) + 2\sin t\cdot\cos t = 0$$

  3. Check: $z(t) = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$ is constant.

What the example shows

The point moves along a level curve, the velocity is perpendicular to the gradient, and their dot product is zero (choose “circle” in Figure 3).

Example 8two variablestree

A tree: $z = uv$, $u = x + y$, $v = x - y$

The goal

Find the partial derivatives via the dependency tree and check them by substitution.

  1. The edges of the tree.

    $$z_u = v,\quad z_v = u;\qquad u_x = 1,\ u_y = 1;\qquad v_x = 1,\ v_y = -1$$

  2. Two paths to $x$, two paths to $y$.

    $$z_x = v\cdot 1 + u\cdot 1 = 2x,\qquad z_y = v\cdot 1 + u\cdot(-1) = -2y$$

  3. Check: $z = (x + y)(x - y) = x^2 - y^2$.

    $$z_x = 2x,\qquad z_y = -2y$$
What the example shows

The sum over paths gives exactly the same as differentiating after substitution, but does not require it, which matters when the substitution is impossible or too cumbersome.

Example 9total derivative

Partial versus total: $z = xy$, $y = x^2$

The goal

Get a feel for the difference between $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{dz}{dx}$.

  1. Partial: $y$ is a constant.

    $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = y = x^2$$

  2. Total: the direct path plus the path through $y$.

    $$\frac{dz}{dx} = y + x\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2 + x\cdot 2x = 3x^2$$

  3. Check: $z = x^3$.

    $$\frac{dz}{dx} = 3x^2$$
What the example shows

The partial derivative answers the question “what if only $x$ changes”. The total derivative answers “what actually happens” when a change in $x$ drags $y$ along with it.

Example 10neural networkbackward pass

The gradient of a neuron with cross-entropy

The goal

Go through the chain $L \leftarrow a \leftarrow z \leftarrow (w, b)$ and obtain the famous short gradient formula.

  1. The links: $z = wx + b$, $a = \sigma(z)$, $L = -\big(y\ln a + (1 - y)\ln(1 - a)\big)$.

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = -\frac{y}{a} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - a} = \frac{a - y}{a(1 - a)},\qquad \frac{da}{dz} = a(1 - a)$$

  2. The product: the factor $a(1 - a)$ cancels.

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{a - y}{a(1 - a)}\cdot a(1 - a) = a - y$$

  3. The last link.

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = (a - y)\,x,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = a - y$$
What the example shows

The chain rule explains why the sigmoid is used together with cross-entropy: their derivatives cancel, and the gradient is simply the “error” $a - y$ times the input.

    $$f'(x) = e^{3x}\cdot\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{e^{3h} - 1}{h} = 3e^{3x}$$

  4. Check with the chain rule: the answer is the same.

    $$\big(e^u\big)' = e^u\cdot u' = e^{3x}\cdot 3$$
Answer
$$f'(x) = 3e^{3x}$$

Find the derivative from the definition, then check it with the chain rule.

$$f(x) = \sin 2x$$
Solution · 5 steps

  1. Write the definition of the derivative: the limit of the difference quotient as $h \to 0$.

    $$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(2x + 2h) - \sin 2x}{h}$$

  2. Substitute $f(x + h)$ and $f(x)$ and expand the difference in the numerator.

    $$\sin(2x + 2h) - \sin 2x = 2\cos(2x + h)\,\sin h$$

  3. Use a standard limit (it is proved once, separately).

    $$\frac{2\cos(2x + h)\sin h}{h} = 2\cos(2x + h)\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h},\qquad \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1$$

    $$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} 2\cos(2x + h)\cdot\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 2\cos 2x\cdot 1 = 2\cos 2x$$

  5. Check with the chain rule: the answer is the same.

    $$\big(\sin u\big)' = \cos u\cdot u' = 2\cos 2x$$
Answer
$$f'(x) = 2\cos 2x$$

Find the partial derivatives from the definition.

$$f(x, y) = (x + y)^2$$
Solution · 2 steps

    $$\begin{aligned}f_x &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\big((x + h) + y\big)^2 - (x + y)^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{2(x + y)h + h^2}{h}\\ &= \lim_{h \to 0}\big(2(x + y) + h\big) = 2(x + y)\end{aligned}$$

Answer
$$f_x = f_y = 2(x + y)$$

The multivariable chain rule

№ 44★★★★★

Find $\frac{dz}{dt}$ by the multivariable chain rule.

$$z = x^2 y,\quad x = \cos t,\quad y = \sin t$$
Solution · 6 steps

  1. Draw the dependency tree: several paths lead from $z$ to the variable we differentiate by.

    $$z \to (x,\ y),\qquad x \to t,\quad y \to t$$

  2. The partial derivatives of the outer function with respect to the intermediate variables.

    $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 2xy,\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = x^2$$

  3. The derivatives of the intermediate variables.

    $$\frac{dx}{dt} = -\sin t,\qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = \cos t$$

  4. The multivariable chain rule: multiply the derivatives along each path and add the contributions of all paths.

    $$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} = 2xy(-\sin t) + x^2\cos t$$

  5. Express the intermediate variables through the original ones and simplify.

    $$= -2\cos t\sin^2 t + \cos^3 t$$

  6. Check: substitute first, then differentiate as a function of one variable — the answer matches.

    $$z = \cos^2 t\,\sin t;\quad z' = -2\cos t\sin t\cdot\sin t + \cos^2 t\cos t$$
Answer
$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \cos^3 t - 2\cos t\sin^2 t$$

Test yourself

1. What is the derivative of $y = \cos(4x)$?
The outer $\cos u$ gives $-\sin u$, the inner $u = 4x$ gives a factor of $4$; the outer derivative is evaluated at $u = 4x$.
2. Which function is the outer one in $y = \ln^3 x$?
$\ln^3 x = (\ln x)^3$: the last operation is cubing. $y' = 3\ln^2 x\cdot\frac1x$.
3. What is the limit $\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h}$?
$\frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h \to 2x$: this is the derivative of $x^2$ from the definition.
4. $z = f(x, y)$, $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$. What is $\frac{dz}{dt}$?
There are two paths from $z$ to $t$, through $x$ and through $y$; along a path the derivatives multiply, and the contributions of the paths add up.
5. $z = x + y$, $y = x^2$. What are $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{dz}{dx}$?
The partial derivative holds $y$ constant: $1$. The total derivative accounts for the path through $y$: $1 + 1\cdot 2x$.
6. Why is the naive proof $\frac{\Delta y}{h} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{h}$ not rigorous?
If $\Delta u = 0$, the fraction $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}$ is undefined. The rigorous proof gets around this with the auxiliary function $\varphi$.
