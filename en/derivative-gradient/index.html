The problem: steepness at a single point

Steepness answers the question “how much did we climb while moving forward?” Between two points it is easy to compute: divide the rise by the distance travelled. But often we need the steepness at a single point: the speed of a car at this very moment, how fast a neural network’s error grows at a given value of a weight, the slope of the hillside right where we are standing. At a single point, though, we have not moved anywhere, and the formula “rise ÷ distance” turns into nonsense:

$$\text{steepness} = \frac{0}{0}\ \ \longrightarrow\ \ \text{undefined}$$

The way out, found by Newton and Leibniz and made rigorous by Cauchy and Weierstrass, is to take a small step, compute the ratio and see what it tends to as the step shrinks. The step is never zero, and if the function is “smooth” at the point, the ratio converges to a definite number. That number is the derivative. The whole article develops this single idea:

Increment: how much a quantity has changed

An increment is the difference between the new and the old value of a quantity, that is, how much it has changed:

$$\Delta x = x_{\text{new}} - x_{\text{old}}$$

The Greek letter $\Delta$ (“delta”) reads as “change in”. $\Delta x$ is a single number, not the product of $\Delta$ and $x$. An increment can be positive (the quantity grew), negative (it decreased) or zero.

The definition of the derivative involves two increments:

$\Delta x$
the increment of the argument: how far we moved $x$. We choose it ourselves; it is the cause
$\Delta y = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)$
the increment of the function (also written $\Delta f$): how much the value of the function changed in response. It is the effect
$\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$
the average rate of change of the function between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$: how many times larger the change in the function is than the change in the argument

Example. $f(x) = x^2$: we were at the point $x = 3$ and moved to $3.1$. Then $\Delta x = 3.1 - 3 = 0.1$, and $\Delta y = 3.1^2 - 3^2 = 9.61 - 9 = 0.61$. The ratio $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{0.61}{0.1} = 6.1$ says that on this interval the function changed, on average, $6.1$ times faster than the argument. As $\Delta x$ shrinks, this ratio approaches $6$, which is exactly $f'(3) = 2\cdot 3$ (we will prove this below).

An everyday analogy

$\Delta x$ is how much time has passed, $\Delta y$ is how many kilometres you have driven, $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ is the average speed over that stretch, and the derivative is the speedometer reading at this moment. The speedometer shows what the average speed tends to as you take shorter and shorter time intervals.

The slope of a line: rise ÷ run

Slope answers the question: how much do I climb when I take one step to the right? For a straight line it takes three steps to compute:

  1. Pick two points on the line, any two. For example, $A = (0,\ 1)$ and $B = (2,\ 4)$: the first number is $x$ (to the right), the second is $y$ (up).
  2. Count how far we moved right and how far we climbed. Right: $\Delta x = 2 - 0 = 2$. Up: $\Delta y = 4 - 1 = 3$.
  3. Divide the rise by the run: $k = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5$.

The meaning of the number $1.5$: for every unit step to the right, the line rises by one and a half units. Check with other points of the same line $y = 1.5x + 1$: $A = (0,\ 1)$, $B = (4,\ 7)$: right $4$, up $6$, slope $6 \div 4 = 1.5$; $A = (-2,\ -2)$, $B = (1,\ 2.5)$: right $3$, up $4.5$, slope $4.5 \div 3 = 1.5$. A straight line has the same slope for any pair of points, which is why we can call it the slope of the line.

Why this does not work for a curve

If we take a parabola instead of a line and do the same thing (two points, rise ÷ run), we get the slope of a secant, and it depends on which two points we took. To get the slope at a single point, we move the second point towards the first. That is the next section.

Secant, tangent and the “tail” of the increment

A secant (from the Latin secare, “to cut”) is a line through two points of a curve: it cuts through the graph, entering at one place and leaving at another. Its slope $\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}$ is the average steepness over the interval. A tangent (from the Latin tangere, “to touch”) is a line that touches the curve at one point and follows its direction there, like a ruler held against the side of a ball (at other points the tangent may cross the curve). Its slope is the derivative, the steepness at the point.

Infinitely many lines pass through a single point, so the tangent cannot be constructed “directly”. It is obtained as a limit: take the secant through two points and move the second point towards the first. As the step $\Delta x$ tends to zero, the secant rotates and settles onto the tangent.

Breaking the increment into parts

Take $f(x) = x^2$ at the point $x = 1$ and write out the increment exactly, with no approximations:

$$\Delta f = (1 + \Delta x)^2 - 1^2 = \underbrace{2\cdot\Delta x}_{\text{linear part}} + \underbrace{\Delta x^2}_{\text{tail}}$$

(Here $\Delta x^2$ means $(\Delta x)^2$, not the increment of $x^2$.) The two terms behave differently. The linear part $2\,\Delta x$ shrinks in proportion to the step. The tail $\Delta x^2$ shrinks much faster: when the step becomes $10$ times smaller, the tail becomes $100$ times smaller. So for small steps the tail becomes negligible compared with the linear part, and the ratio $\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = 2 + \Delta x$ tends to the coefficient of the linear part, that is, to $2$.

The point in one sentence

The coefficient of the linear part of the increment is the derivative. “An infinitesimal $dx$” is a colloquial reminder that for small steps the tail can be neglected; strictly speaking, $dx$ is simply $\Delta x$, and $dy = f'(x)\,dx$ is the linear part of the increment (see the section on the differential below).

The definition of the derivative

Now we can give a rigorous definition. Let the function $y = f(x)$ be defined in some neighbourhood of the point $x$. First we introduce the increment of the function, and then the definition is written as a chain of equalities:

$$\Delta y = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)$$
$$f'(x) = \frac{dy}{dx} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}$$

The derivative of a function $f$ at a point $x$ is the limit of the ratio of the increment of the function to the increment of the argument as the increment of the argument tends to zero, provided this limit exists and is finite. The function is then called differentiable at the point $x$.

The algorithm for computing from the definition

  1. Write down $f(x + \Delta x)$: substitute $x + \Delta x$ for every $x$.
  2. Find the increment $\Delta f = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)$ and simplify it so that a factor of $\Delta x$ appears (expand the brackets, bring to a common denominator, multiply by the conjugate, apply a trigonometric identity).
  3. Divide by $\Delta x$ and cancel. This is legitimate: in the definition of the limit $\Delta x \ne 0$.
  4. Pass to the limit $\Delta x \to 0$: if the remaining expression is continuous in $\Delta x$, simply substitute $\Delta x = 0$; otherwise use a known limit.

Example: $f(x) = x^2$ at any point. $\Delta f = (x + \Delta x)^2 - x^2 = 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2$; $\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = 2x + \Delta x$; $f'(x) = \lim\limits_{\Delta x \to 0}(2x + \Delta x) = 2x$. At $x = 1$ we get $2$, at $x = 3$ we get $6$, just as in the tables above.

The letter of the variable does not matter: $\frac{df}{dx}$, $\frac{df}{dz}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$

The notation $\frac{df}{dz}$ means the same operation as $\frac{df}{dx}$, except that the variable is called $z$. The notation $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ (the partial derivative, see below) is the same operation for a function of several variables, with the other variables frozen. Compare:

What the derivative means

  • Geometrically, it is the slope of the tangent to the graph at the point. The equation of the tangent: $y = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,(x - x_0)$. For $f(x) = x^2$ at $x_0 = 1$: $y = 1 + 2(x - 1) = 2x - 1$.
  • Physically, it is the instantaneous rate of change: if $s(t)$ is distance, then $s'(t)$ is velocity; if $v(t)$ is velocity, then $v'(t)$ is acceleration.
  • The sign: $f'(x) > 0$ means the function is increasing at this point (a step to the right is a climb), $f'(x) < 0$ means it is decreasing, $f'(x) = 0$ means the tangent is horizontal (a candidate for a minimum or a maximum).
  • The magnitude: the larger $|f'(x)|$, the steeper the graph and the more strongly the function responds to a small change in the argument.

What dx and df are: the differential and linear approximation

In the 19th century Cauchy and Weierstrass removed “infinitesimals” as objects in their own right; only the notion of a limit remained. An important consequence: $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is a single symbol, a single number, not a fraction of two quantities. $dx$ “on its own” plays no part in the definition of the derivative. (Below, once we introduce differentials, we will see that for $dx \ne 0$ this equality also holds as the quotient of $dy$ by $dx$.)

Nevertheless, the symbols $dx$ and $dy$ are given a precise meaning through the differential. The differential of a function is the linear part of the increment:

$$dy = f'(x)\,dx,\qquad dx = \Delta x$$

Here $dx$ is the same increment of the argument $\Delta x$ (an arbitrary number), and $dy$ is the rise along the tangent, not along the graph. The exact increment $\Delta y$ differs from $dy$ by the tail, which is small even compared with $\Delta x$:

$$\Delta y = \underbrace{f'(x)\,\Delta x}_{dy} + \underbrace{\varepsilon(\Delta x)\,\Delta x}_{\text{tail}},\qquad \varepsilon(\Delta x) \to 0$$

This gives the linear approximation: $f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x$. For example, $\sqrt{4.1} \approx \sqrt4 + \frac{1}{2\sqrt4}\cdot 0.1 = 2 + 0.025 = 2.025$ (the exact value is $2.02485\ldots$). When people write $df = 2\,dx$, they mean: “$\Delta f \approx 2\,\Delta x$, and the smaller $\Delta x$, the better the approximation”.

How a computer computes a derivative

A computer cannot take a limit. It takes a finite but small step $h$ (our $\Delta x$) and gets an approximation. The one-sided difference $\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$ is off by an amount of order $h$, the central difference $\frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}$ by an amount of order $h^2$, because the tails on the right and on the left cancel each other out. This is how ML practitioners check that gradient formulas are correct (gradient check).

pythonimport numpy as np

# The derivative from the definition on a computer: we cannot take a limit,
# so we take a small but finite step h (this is Δx)
def derivative(f, x, h=1e-5):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)   # central difference: error ~ h²

print(derivative(lambda x: x**2, 1.0))      # ≈ 2.0000000000  (exactly: 2)

# Partial derivatives: only one variable gets the increment
def gradient(f, p, h=1e-5):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    g = np.zeros_like(p)
    for i in range(len(p)):
        e = np.zeros_like(p); e[i] = h        # shift only the i-th coordinate
        g[i] = (f(p + e) - f(p - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

f = lambda p: p[0]**2 + p[1]**2
print(gradient(f, [3, 4]))                 # ≈ [6. 8.]

# Gradient descent: a step against the gradient
p, eta = np.array([3.0, 4.0]), 0.1
for k in range(1, 4):
    p = p - eta * gradient(f, p)
    print(k, p.round(4), round(f(p), 4))    # 1 [2.4 3.2] 16.0 · 2 [1.92 2.56] 10.24 · 3 [1.536 2.048] 6.5536

When the derivative does not exist

The definition requires the limit to exist and be finite, and to be the same whichever side $\Delta x$ approaches zero from. This is not always the case.

A kink: $f(x) = |x|$ at the point $0$. $\frac{f(0 + \Delta x) - f(0)}{\Delta x} = \frac{|\Delta x|}{\Delta x}$. On the right ($\Delta x > 0$) this is $1$, on the left ($\Delta x < 0$) it is $-1$. The one-sided limits differ, there is no common limit, so there is no derivative at zero. Geometrically: at a kink there is no single tangent. (The ReLU function in neural networks is built the same way; at zero it is simply assigned the derivative $0$ or $1$.)

A vertical tangent: $f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}$ at zero. $\frac{\sqrt[3]{\Delta x}}{\Delta x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{\Delta x^2}} \to +\infty$: the ratio grows without bound, and there is no finite limit.

A discontinuity. If a function is discontinuous at a point, it has no derivative there. Let us prove an equivalent statement from the definition: if $f$ is differentiable at a point $x$, then it is continuous there. Indeed,

$$f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) = \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}\cdot\Delta x\ \xrightarrow[\Delta x \to 0]{}\ f'(x)\cdot 0 = 0$$

that is, $f(x + \Delta x) \to f(x)$. The converse is false: $|x|$ is continuous at zero but not differentiable.

A subtle example

The function $f(x) = x^2\sin\frac1x$ (with $f(0) = 0$) oscillates infinitely often near zero, but by the definition $\frac{f(\Delta x) - 0}{\Delta x} = \Delta x\sin\frac{1}{\Delta x} \to 0$ (a bounded factor times an infinitesimal). So $f'(0) = 0$ exists. The definition is more reliable than intuition “by eye”.

The derivative in motion: 7 animations from simple to advanced

Animation 1. A walker and a car: the same time, different speeds

Two people leave work and head home. The walker lives $400$ m away and walks steadily at $80$ m/min. The driver lives $3$ km away: the car speeds up, drives fast and brakes near home (its distance is $s(t) = 3000\,(3u^2 - 2u^3)$, $u = t/5$, and its speed is $s'(t) = 144\,t\,(5 - t)$ m/min). Both are home exactly $5$ minutes later. The time is the same, but the speeds are completely different — and not only on average, but at almost every moment: the instantaneous speeds coincide only twice — while the car is speeding up (at about $t \approx 0.11$ min) and while it is braking (at about $t \approx 4.89$ min).

What we see. The walker’s distance graph is a straight line: every minute they cover the same $80$ m, the slope is the same everywhere, and the average speed equals the instantaneous one. The car’s graph is a curve: gentle at first (speeding up), steep in the middle (driving fast), gentle again at the end (braking). The car’s average speed is $3000 \div 5 = 600$ m/min, but in the middle of the trip ($t = 2.5$ min) the speedometer shows $900$ m/min ($54$ km/h), close to the start and near home almost zero, and at the very moment of departure and arrival exactly zero. The instantaneous speed is the slope of the tangent, that is, the derivative $s'(t)$.

What the animation showed

The average speed answers the question “how much on average over the whole trip” and describes very different trips in the same way. The derivative answers the question “how fast right now” — and tells them apart at every moment.

Animation 2. The average speed turns into the speedometer reading

How can we know the speed “right now” if by definition speed is distance divided by time, and in an instant the distance is zero? The answer is a limit: we take the interval $[t_0,\ t_0 + \Delta t]$, compute the average speed $\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$ and shrink $\Delta t$.

The $\Delta t$ slider is logarithmic: from $2.5$ minutes down to $0.005$ minutes ($0.3$ s). While the interval is large, the average speed can differ a lot from the instantaneous one (the car has time both to speed up and to brake). When the interval is tiny, the speed barely changes on it, and by taking $\Delta t$ small enough, one can make the difference between the average and the instantaneous speed as small as desired. This is exactly the formula $s'(t_0) = \lim\limits_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{s(t_0 + \Delta t) - s(t_0)}{\Delta t}$ — an electronic speedometer essentially does just that: it counts the wheel’s revolutions over very short time spans.

What the animation showed

The derivative is not “speed over zero time” but the number the average speed approaches as the time interval gets shorter and shorter. The secant turns and lies down on the tangent.

Animation 3. Filling a vessel: how fast the water rises

Water is poured into a vessel at a constant rate $Q$ (the same volume every second). The level $h(t)$, however, rises unevenly: in the same second the water climbs high in a narrow part and barely moves in a wide one. The rate at which it rises is the derivative of the level with respect to time.

Why. During a short time $\Delta t$ a volume $Q\,\Delta t$ flows in, and it settles as a thin layer with the water surface area $A(h)$ and thickness $\Delta h$: $Q\,\Delta t \approx A(h)\,\Delta h$. Divide by $\Delta t$ and pass to the limit:

$$\frac{dh}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta h}{\Delta t} = \frac{Q}{A(h)}$$

For a cylinder $A$ is constant, and the graph of $h(t)$ is a straight line. In a funnel that widens upwards the water rises fast at first and then more and more slowly — the graph is concave down. In a vase the speed first falls and then rises again: fast in the narrow base, slow in the wide “belly” (around $h \approx 6$ cm), fast again near the narrowing top; the graph of $h(t)$ is first concave down, and after the widest point concave up.

What the animation showed

The derivative is the rate of change of a quantity, and it can depend on the state of the system itself: how fast the water rises is determined by the width of the vessel at the current level. The shape of the graph of $h(t)$ “tells” the shape of the vessel — through its slope.

Animation 4. The microscope: a curve becomes a straight line

Why can we speak of the slope of a curve at a point at all? Because a smooth curve looks like a straight line near a point. Let us take a “microscope” and magnify the neighbourhood of the point $x_0$, keeping the same scale on both axes.

For $x^2$ and $\sin x$ at $1000\times$ magnification the curve can no longer be told apart from the tangent: the deviation itself is of the order of the square of the window size (this is the second-order “tail” from the section on the secant), so the deviation divided by the window width falls in proportion to the window size. The slope of this line is the derivative $f'(x_0)$. For $|x|$ at zero the picture does not change at any magnification: two rays with slopes $-1$ and $+1$. There is no line the curve “converges” to — and no derivative.

What the animation showed

A function is differentiable at a point if under a microscope it becomes a non-vertical straight line there. The derivative is the slope of that line. At a kink or a discontinuity the microscope shows no line, and with a vertical tangent the line is vertical — its slope is infinite, and there is no derivative.

Animation 5. Building the graph of the derivative from slopes

The derivative at each point is a number (the slope of the tangent). If we record this number for every $x$, we get a new function $f'(x)$ with its own graph. Let us build it live.

For $x^3 - 3x$ everything is visible at once: at the edges the function rises steeply — $f'$ is large and positive; towards the peak at $x = -1$ the rise slows down, and $f'$ drops to zero; then the function decreases — $f' < 0$; at the trough at $x = 1$ again $f' = 0$, and then it rises. The lower graph is the parabola $3x^2 - 3$: by the definition (practice, No. 6) $(x^3)' = 3x^2$, $(3x)' = 3$. For $\sin x$ the lower graph is the cosine, for $x^2$ the line $2x$.

What the animation showed

The sign of the derivative shows whether the function increases or decreases, and the zeros of the derivative are candidates for maxima and minima. From the graph of $f'$ one can “read” the behaviour of $f$ without seeing $f$ itself.

Animation 6. A thrown ball: height, velocity, acceleration

A ball is thrown upwards at $20$ m/s (we take the acceleration due to gravity to be $10$ m/s²). Its height is $h(t) = 20t - 5t^2$. The derivative can be taken once more: the derivative of height is velocity, the derivative of velocity is acceleration.

By the definition: $\frac{h(t + \Delta t) - h(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{20\Delta t - 10t\,\Delta t - 5\Delta t^2}{\Delta t} = 20 - 10t - 5\Delta t \to 20 - 10t = v(t)$. Once more: $\frac{v(t + \Delta t) - v(t)}{\Delta t} = -10 \to -10 = a$. While the ball flies up, $v > 0$ and decreasing; at the top, at $t = 2$ s, the velocity is zero (the tangent to the height graph is horizontal) and the height is at its maximum — $20$ m; then $v < 0$ and the ball falls. The acceleration is the same all the time: $-10$ m/s² (the minus sign is there because it points downwards; we neglect air resistance).

What the animation showed

The derivative of the derivative — the second derivative $h''$ — shows how the rate of change itself changes: here it is the acceleration. Here the maximum height is where the first derivative becomes zero and changes sign from “+” to “−”.

Animation 7. Gears: why derivatives multiply in the chain rule

A composite function $y = f(u)$, $u = g(x)$ is a transmission of two pairs of gears. The derivative of each link is its “gear ratio”: how many times faster the output of the link changes than its input (in engineering, the term “gear ratio” often means the reciprocal).

If $u$ changes $2$ times faster than $x$, and $y$ changes $1.5$ times faster than $u$, then $y$ changes $2\cdot 1.5 = 3$ times faster than $x$. This is the chain rule $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$ — for nonlinear functions the gear ratios depend on the point: $\frac{du}{dx}$ is taken at $x$, and $\frac{dy}{du}$ at $u = g(x)$. A detailed derivation, walkthroughs and practice are in the article “The chain rule”.

What the animation showed

The derivative is the stretch factor of small changes. When a change passes through several links in a row, the factors multiply.

The table of derivatives: every row from the definition

The table of derivatives is not a set of rules “to memorise” but a summary of results, each obtained from the definition. Below are the derivations; the numbers refer to the detailed solutions in the practice section.

Powers: the binomial theorem

For $f(x) = x^n$ ($n$ a natural number), the binomial theorem gives $(x + \Delta x)^n = x^n + n x^{n-1}\Delta x + C_n^2 x^{n-2}\Delta x^2 + \ldots + \Delta x^n$. Therefore

$$\frac{(x + \Delta x)^n - x^n}{\Delta x} = n x^{n-1} + C_n^2 x^{n-2}\Delta x + \ldots + \Delta x^{n-1}\ \xrightarrow[\Delta x \to 0]{}\ n x^{n-1}$$

Every term except the first contains $\Delta x$ and vanishes. Hence $(x^2)' = 2x$, $(x^3)' = 3x^2$, $(x^4)' = 4x^3$ (practice, No. 3, 6, 9). A constant: $\Delta f = c - c = 0$, so $(c)' = 0$ (No. 1).

Root and fraction: the conjugate and a common denominator ($x > 0$ and $x \ne 0$)

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{\sqrt{x + \Delta x} - \sqrt{x}}{\Delta x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + \Delta x} + \sqrt{x}} \to \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\\ \frac{1}{\Delta x}\Big(\frac{1}{x + \Delta x} - \frac1x\Big) = -\frac{1}{x(x + \Delta x)} \to -\frac{1}{x^2}\end{gathered}$$

Exponential, logarithm, sine: the fundamental limits and their corollaries

Here the increment does not expand into a polynomial, and the last step needs one of the fundamental limits; they are proved once, separately: $\lim\limits_{t \to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1$, $\lim\limits_{t \to 0}\frac{e^t - 1}{t} = 1$, $\lim\limits_{t \to 0}\frac{\ln(1 + t)}{t} = 1$.

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{e^{x + \Delta x} - e^x}{\Delta x} = e^x\,\frac{e^{\Delta x} - 1}{\Delta x} \to e^x\\ \frac{\sin(x + \Delta x) - \sin x}{\Delta x} = \cos\!\Big(x + \frac{\Delta x}{2}\Big)\frac{\sin(\Delta x/2)}{\Delta x/2} \to \cos x\ \ (\cos\ \text{is continuous})\end{gathered}$$

The differentiation rules, also from the definition

Sum and constant factor. $\frac{\Delta(f + g)}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} + \frac{\Delta g}{\Delta x} \to f' + g'$ and $\frac{\Delta(cf)}{\Delta x} = c\,\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} \to c f'$: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits.

Product. Add and subtract $f(x)\,g(x + \Delta x)$:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{f(x + \Delta x)g(x + \Delta x) - f(x)g(x)}{\Delta x} &= \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x}\,g(x + \Delta x) + f(x)\,\frac{\Delta g}{\Delta x}\\ &\xrightarrow[\Delta x \to 0]{}\ f'g + fg'\end{aligned}$$

So all the differentiation rules are consequences of a single definition. Some follow from it directly (the constant, the sum, the constant factor, a natural power, the product), others are more convenient to obtain from rules already proved: the quotient from the product and the derivative of $\frac1g$, a power with an arbitrary exponent via $x^a = e^{a\ln x}$ and the chain rule. But in the end everything rests on the same limit.

(here $g(x + \Delta x) \to g(x)$ because a differentiable function is continuous). The quotient rule $\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$ is derived in the same way, and the derivative of a composite function $(f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ is covered in detail in the article “The chain rule”.

Partial derivatives: freeze every variable but one

Suppose a function depends on several variables: $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ is the height of a bowl above the point $(x, y)$; the loss function of a neural network depends on millions of weights. Such a function has no single derivative: from the point $(x, y)$ you can move in infinitely many directions, and each has its own slope. Let us start with the simplest directions, along the axes. To do that, all variables except one are frozen.

A partial derivative is the same definition of the derivative, except that only one variable gets an increment and the others are treated as constants. For a function of two variables:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{f(x + \Delta x,\ y) - f(x,\ y)}{\Delta x}$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0}\frac{f(x,\ y + \Delta y) - f(x,\ y)}{\Delta y}$$

In terms of increments this looks just like the one-variable case, except that the increment of the function is taken with respect to one variable; this is the partial increment:

$$\Delta_x f = f(x + \Delta x,\ y) - f(x,\ y),\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{\Delta_x f}{\Delta x}$$
$$\Delta_y f = f(x,\ y + \Delta y) - f(x,\ y),\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0}\frac{\Delta_y f}{\Delta y}$$

Textbooks often denote the increment by the letter $h$; it is the same $\Delta x$:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x + h,\ y) - f(x,\ y)}{h}\\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x,\ y + h) - f(x,\ y)}{h}\end{gathered}$$

In general, for $f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ with respect to the variable $x_i$:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = \lim_{\Delta x_i \to 0}\frac{f(x_1, \ldots, x_i + \Delta x_i, \ldots, x_n) - f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)}{\Delta x_i}$$

Example from the definition: $f(x, y) = x^2 y + y^3$. With respect to $x$: $\Delta_x f = (x + \Delta x)^2 y + y^3 - x^2 y - y^3 = (2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2)\,y$; the term $y^3$ does not depend on $x$ and cancels; $\frac{\Delta_x f}{\Delta x} = (2x + \Delta x)\,y \to 2xy$. With respect to $y$: $\Delta_y f = x^2\Delta y + 3y^2\Delta y + 3y\,\Delta y^2 + \Delta y^3$; $\frac{\Delta_y f}{\Delta y} \to x^2 + 3y^2$. So $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy$ ($y$ behaves like a number, $y^3$ gives zero) and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 3y^2$ ($x$ behaves like a number).

A real-life example: a shower with two taps

The water temperature in a shower depends on two variables at once. Hot water at $60\ ^\circ$C flows at a rate of $h$ l/min, cold water at $10\ ^\circ$C at a rate of $c$ l/min. The temperature of the mix is the average weighted by the flow rates:

$$T(h, c) = \frac{60h + 10c}{h + c},\qquad T(2,\ 3) = \frac{120 + 30}{5} = 30\ ^\circ\text{C}$$

Freezing. Want to understand how each tap affects the result? If you turn both at once, you cannot tell what changed the temperature. So you do what everyone does in the shower: take your hand off the cold tap ($c$ is frozen) and turn only the hot one a little. That is exactly a partial derivative. By the definition, only $h$ gets an increment:

$$\Delta_h T = \frac{60(h + \Delta h) + 10c}{h + \Delta h + c} - \frac{60h + 10c}{h + c} = \frac{50c\,\Delta h}{(h + \Delta h + c)(h + c)}$$
$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial h} = \lim_{\Delta h \to 0}\frac{50c}{(h + \Delta h + c)(h + c)} = \frac{50c}{(h + c)^2} = \frac{50\cdot 3}{25} = 6$$

Each extra litre per minute of hot water gives about $+6\ ^\circ$C, as long as the cold tap is left alone. Conversely, freezing the hot tap and turning the cold one, we get in the same way $\frac{\partial T}{\partial c} = -\frac{50h}{(h + c)^2} = -\frac{50\cdot 2}{25} = -4$: each litre per minute of cold water gives about $-4\ ^\circ$C; the minus means that this tap cools.

If you turn both. Add $0.1$ l/min on each tap and the effects add up (the total differential): $dT \approx 6\cdot 0.1 + (-4)\cdot 0.1 = 0.2\ ^\circ$C. A check with the exact formula: $T(2.1,\ 3.1) = \frac{157}{5.2} \approx 30.19\ ^\circ$C; it matches.

  • Freezing is needed to isolate the contribution of one “knob”. It is the same principle as in an experiment: change one factor and keep the rest constant.
  • A partial derivative depends on where exactly the others are frozen. If the cold tap is almost closed ($c \approx 0$), then $\frac{\partial T}{\partial h} = \frac{50c}{(h + c)^2} \approx 0$: the water is almost all hot anyway, and the hot tap no longer changes the temperature.
  • The gradient $\nabla T = (6,\ -4)$ tells you how to warm the water fastest: add hot, reduce cold, in the proportion $6$ to $4$.

The same with a car. The braking distance depends on the speed $v$ and on the grip of the tyres on the road $\mu$: $s(v, \mu) = \frac{v^2}{2\mu g}$. The partial derivative $\frac{\partial s}{\partial v} = \frac{v}{\mu g}$ answers the question “the road is the same, but I am driving faster: how much longer is the distance?”, and $\frac{\partial s}{\partial \mu} = -\frac{v^2}{2\mu^2 g}$ answers “the speed is the same, but it started raining: how much longer is the distance?”. With $v = 20$ m/s ($72$ km/h), $\mu = 0.8$, $g = 9.8$ m/s²: $s \approx 25.5$ m, $\frac{\partial s}{\partial v} \approx 2.55$ m per m/s, $\frac{\partial s}{\partial \mu} \approx -31.9$ m per unit of $\mu$; on a wet road ($\mu = 0.5$) the distance grows to $\approx 40.8$ m. In real life both change, but to understand the contribution of each factor, they are considered separately.

Partial derivatives are not the whole story

Partial derivatives describe the slope only along the axes. The slope in any other direction and the change in the function when all variables shift at once are obtained from them through the gradient and the total differential, provided the function is differentiable (see the sections below).

The gradient and nabla: all partial derivatives in one vector

Let us collect the partial derivatives into one vector, the gradient:

$$\nabla f(x, y) = \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Big)$$

Expanding each component by the definition, we get the gradient as a vector of two limits:

$$\nabla f(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \displaystyle\lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{f(x + \Delta x,\ y) - f(x,\ y)}{\Delta x}\\[4mm] \displaystyle\lim_{\Delta y \to 0}\frac{f(x,\ y + \Delta y) - f(x,\ y)}{\Delta y} \end{pmatrix}$$

The two rows here are two components of one object, not two equations for one quantity. That is why in applied texts the notation $f'(x, y)$ is usually not used for a function of two variables: it has no single derivative-number, and the role of the “full” derivative is played by the gradient vector. (In rigorous analysis $f'$ denotes a linear map, the row $(f_x,\ f_y)$, which is essentially the same gradient.)

FunctionDerivative

$n$ variables$f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$$\nabla f$, a vector of $n$ numbers

The gradient of a function of one variable is a vector with one component, and that component coincides with the ordinary derivative: $\nabla f(x) = \big(\frac{df}{dx}\big) = f'(x)$. The ordinary derivative is the special case of the gradient for $n = 1$; in one dimension we simply say “derivative”. The meaning carries over: the sign of $f'(x)$ shows the direction of growth ($f' > 0$: to the right, $f' < 0$: to the left), and a line has only two directions, so one signed number is enough for the “vector”.

Nabla is the operator, the gradient is the result

The symbol $\nabla$ (“nabla”) is an operator: it takes a function and returns the vector of its partial derivatives. For a function of three variables $f(x, y, z)$:

$$\nabla = \Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial}{\partial y},\ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big),\qquad \nabla f = \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\Big) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,\mathbf{k}$$

Here $\mathbf{i} = (1,\ 0,\ 0)$, $\mathbf{j} = (0,\ 1,\ 0)$, $\mathbf{k} = (0,\ 0,\ 1)$ are the unit vectors along the $x$, $y$, $z$ axes. Any vector can be expanded in terms of them: $(2,\ 5,\ -3) = 2\mathbf{i} + 5\mathbf{j} - 3\mathbf{k}$. So both ways of writing the gradient mean the same thing; in ML people usually just write the list of components, while $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ are more common in physics. As many variables, as many components: for the loss function of a neural network with a million weights, the gradient is a vector of a million numbers.

Example. For $f(x, y, z) = x^2 y + \sin z$, from the definition (as in the section on partial derivatives): $\nabla f = (2xy,\ x^2,\ \cos z)$. For $f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin y$: $\nabla f = (2xy,\ x^2 + \cos y)$, and at the point $(1,\ 0)$ this is $(0,\ 2)$.

The geometric meaning

The gradient is not just “a bunch of numbers” but an arrow on the map. $\nabla f$ points in the direction in which $f$ grows fastest, and its length $|\nabla f|$ is the rate of that growth (we prove this in the next section). Moreover, a nonzero gradient is perpendicular to the level curves, the curves on which $f$ is constant: along a level curve the function does not change, so the slope along it is zero.

The sign of a component and minus nabla

For $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ (a bowl with its bottom at the origin), $\nabla f = (2x,\ 2y)$. At the point $(-1,\ 0.5)$ the gradient is $(-2,\ 1)$, and it is read component by component: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -2$ means that a step to the right makes $f$ fall; $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = +1$ means that a step up makes $f$ grow. At one point a function can fall in one variable and grow in another; the gradient stores exactly this information: for each variable separately, which way and by how much.

Minus nabla $-\nabla f$ is the same vector turned through $180^\circ$: each component is multiplied by $-1$. The length is the same, the direction is opposite: downhill, towards the minimum. For example, $\nabla f(1,\ 2) = (2,\ 4)$, while $-\nabla f(1,\ 2) = (-2,\ -4)$. Gradient descent moves along $-\nabla f$.

The directional derivative

Partial derivatives are slopes along the axes. What is the slope in an arbitrary direction? Let $\mathbf{u} = (u_1,\ u_2)$ be a unit vector ($u_1^2 + u_2^2 = 1$). Move from the point $(x, y)$ by $t$ (a signed amount) along $\mathbf{u}$ and apply the same definition:

$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(x, y) = \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{f(x + t u_1,\ y + t u_2) - f(x,\ y)}{t}$$

For $\mathbf{u} = (1,\ 0)$ this is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, for $\mathbf{u} = (0,\ 1)$ it is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$. Theorem: if $f$ is differentiable, then

$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,u_1 + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,u_2 = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}$$

In $n$ dimensions it is the same for a unit vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_1,\ \ldots,\ v_n)$:

$$D_{\mathbf{v}} f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,v_i$$

Derivation. Differentiability means that the increment is “a plane plus a small error”: $\Delta f = f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y + \varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}$, where $\varepsilon \to 0$ as $(\Delta x, \Delta y) \to (0, 0)$. Substitute $\Delta x = t u_1$, $\Delta y = t u_2$; then $\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2} = |t|$ and

$$\frac{f(x + t u_1,\ y + t u_2) - f(x,\ y)}{t} = f_x u_1 + f_y u_2 + \varepsilon\,\frac{|t|}{t}\ \xrightarrow[t \to 0]{}\ f_x u_1 + f_y u_2$$

Why the gradient is the direction of fastest growth. The dot product equals $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u} = |\nabla f|\,|\mathbf{u}|\cos\varphi = |\nabla f|\cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\nabla f$. It is maximal at $\varphi = 0$ (the direction along the gradient), where it equals $|\nabla f|$; minimal at $\varphi = 180^\circ$ (along $-\nabla f$), where it equals $-|\nabla f|$; and zero at $\varphi = 90^\circ$, along the level curve (all this assuming $\nabla f \ne \mathbf{0}$).

Example from the definition. $f = x^2 + y^2$, the point $(3,\ 4)$, the direction towards the origin $\mathbf{u} = (-0.6,\ -0.8)$: $f(3 - 0.6t,\ 4 - 0.8t) = 25 - 10t + t^2$, the ratio $\frac{-10t + t^2}{t} = -10 + t \to -10$. Via the gradient: $(6,\ 8)\cdot(-0.6,\ -0.8) = -3.6 - 6.4 = -10 = -|\nabla f|$: towards the centre of the bowl the function decreases fastest.

The total differential and linear approximation

How does a function change if we shift all the variables at once by small amounts? The exact change is the total increment:

$$\Delta f = f(x + \Delta x,\ y + \Delta y) - f(x,\ y)$$

Its linear part is the total differential: the contribution of each variable equals its partial derivative times its shift, and the contributions add up:

$$df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy,\qquad df = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i = \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{x}$$

For small shifts $\Delta f \approx df$, and the error shrinks faster than the shifts themselves (for smooth functions it is of at least second order: squares and products of $\Delta x$, $\Delta y$). This is the same linear approximation formula: $f(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y) \approx f(x, y) + f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y$, the tangent plane in place of the surface.

Example: $f(x, y) = x^2 y$ at the point $(2,\ 3)$ with $dx = 0.1$, $dy = 0.2$. From the definition (practice, No. 29), $f_x = 2xy = 12$, $f_y = x^2 = 4$. The differential is $df = 12\cdot 0.1 + 4\cdot 0.2 = 2.0$. The exact increment: $\Delta f = 2.1^2\cdot 3.2 - 2^2\cdot 3 = 14.112 - 12 = 2.112$. The difference is the tail: $y\,\Delta x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x\,\Delta y + \Delta x^2\Delta y = 0.03 + 0.08 + 0.002 = 0.112$.

Partial and total differential

  • A partial differential is the contribution of one variable with the others frozen: $d_x f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx$, $d_y f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy$.
  • The total differential is the sum of all the partial ones: $df = d_x f + d_y f$.
  • For a function of one variable the sum has a single term, and the partial differential coincides with the total one: $dy = f'(x)\,dx$. That is why in one dimension we simply say “differential”, just as we say “derivative” instead of “gradient”.

$\nabla f$ and $df$: a vector and a number

They are easy to confuse because both are built from partial derivatives. $\nabla f$ is a vector, “the steepness of the road”; it exists on its own, without any step: for the bowl it is $(2x,\ 2y)$. $df$ is a number: how much you climbed by taking a specific step $(dx, dy)$: $df = \nabla f\cdot(dx,\ dy) = 2x\,dx + 2y\,dy$. Hence the geometry: for a step of fixed length, $df$ is largest when the step points along $\nabla f$, and zero for a step along a level curve.

Why the existence of partial derivatives is not enough

Take $f(x, y) = \frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ for $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$ and $f(0, 0) = 0$. By the definition, $f_x(0, 0) = \lim\frac{f(\Delta x, 0) - 0}{\Delta x} = \lim\frac{0}{\Delta x} = 0$, and likewise $f_y(0, 0) = 0$. But along the diagonal $y = x$ the function equals $\frac{x^2}{2x^2} = \frac12$: it is not even continuous at zero, and the formula $\Delta f \approx f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y = 0$ fails there.

That is why in several variables one introduces differentiability: $\Delta f = f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y + o\big(\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\big)$. A sufficient condition in practice: the partial derivatives exist and are continuous near the point. This holds for polynomials, exponentials, sines and their combinations at interior points of their domain; roots and absolute values need to be checked.

Gradient descent and finding extrema

Suppose we need to decrease $f$, for example a model’s loss function. The gradient points in the direction of fastest growth, so $-\nabla f$ is the direction of fastest decrease. We take a step against the gradient:

$$\mathbf{p}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{p}_{\text{old}} - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{p}_{\text{old}}),\qquad x \leftarrow x - \eta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\quad y \leftarrow y - \eta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$$

Here $\eta$ (the learning rate) is the step coefficient: the shift is $\eta\,\nabla f$, and its length is $\eta\,|\nabla f|$. At every step the gradient is recomputed: after a move, the slope is different. The gradient is “honest” only locally (it is the linear part, with the tails thrown away), so $\eta$ is kept small.

Example. $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$, start at $(3,\ 4)$, $\eta = 0.1$. $\nabla f(3,\ 4) = (6,\ 8)$, the new point is $(3 - 0.6,\ 4 - 0.8) = (2.4,\ 3.2)$, and $f$ drops from $25$ to $16$. Repeating the steps, we arrive at the minimum at $(0,\ 0)$. For a round bowl the gradient points straight at the minimum; for an elongated bowl such as $x^2 + 10y^2$ it no longer points towards the centre, and the descent starts to “zigzag” across the narrow valley.

In machine learning the variables are the model’s weights $\mathbf{w} = (w_1,\ \ldots,\ w_n)$ and the function is the loss $L$. The descent step for all weights at once and for each weight separately:

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla L(\mathbf{w}),\qquad\text{that is}\qquad w_i \leftarrow w_i - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}$$

Why the step size matters

For the bowl $f = x^2 + y^2$ the step can be computed in general form: $x \leftarrow x - \eta\cdot 2x = (1 - 2\eta)\,x$, and likewise for $y$. Each step multiplies the coordinates by $q = 1 - 2\eta$:

$\eta$$q = 1 - 2\eta$What happens
$0.1$$0.8$slow but steady approach to the bottom
$0.3$$0.4$fast convergence
$0.5$$0$hits the bottom in one step (a coincidence that holds only for this bowl)
$0.8$$-0.6$overshoots the bottom with damped oscillations

In short: descent on this bowl converges if and only if $|1 - 2\eta| < 1$, that is, $0 < \eta < 1$.

Finding extrema: $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$

At an interior minimum or maximum where $f$ is differentiable, the function is “flat” in every direction, so all the partial derivatives are zero. That is why the candidates for extrema are the solutions of the system $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$ and the points where the gradient does not exist (for example, $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ at zero). Example: $f = x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 4y$. From the definition, $f_x = 2x - 2$, $f_y = 2y + 4$; the system $2x - 2 = 0$, $2y + 4 = 0$ gives $(1,\ -2)$, and $f(1,\ -2) = -5$ is the minimum (this is the bowl $(x - 1)^2 + (y + 2)^2 - 5$). The points found are only candidates: each may be a minimum, a maximum or a saddle point (for example, $x^2 - y^2$ at zero; pick this function in Figure 4).

Example 1incrementtable

Increment and average rate: $f(x) = x^2$ near $x = 3$

The goal

See how the ratio of increments tends to the derivative, and find this limit from the definition.

  1. Step $\Delta x = 0.1$: from $3$ to $3.1$.

    $$\Delta y = 3.1^2 - 3^2 = 9.61 - 9 = 0.61,\qquad \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = 6.1$$

  2. A general step $\Delta x$: expand the square.

    $$\Delta y = (3 + \Delta x)^2 - 9 = 6\Delta x + \Delta x^2,\qquad \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = 6 + \Delta x$$

  3. The limit as $\Delta x \to 0$.

    $$f'(3) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}(6 + \Delta x) = 6$$
What the example shows

The average rate $6 + \Delta x$ over the interval differs from the instantaneous rate $6$ by exactly $\Delta x$: this is the “tail” divided by the step.

Example 2from the definitiongeometry

The tangent to the parabola $y = x^2$ at $x_0 = 1$

The goal

Find the derivative from the definition and write the equation of the tangent.

  1. From the definition at the point $1$.

    $$\frac{(1 + \Delta x)^2 - 1}{\Delta x} = \frac{2\Delta x + \Delta x^2}{\Delta x} = 2 + \Delta x \to 2$$

  2. The tangent: the line through $(1,\ 1)$ with slope $2$.

    $$y = f(1) + f'(1)(x - 1) = 1 + 2(x - 1) = 2x - 1$$

  3. Check: the tangent and the parabola differ by exactly the tail.

    $$x^2 - (2x - 1) = (x - 1)^2 = \Delta x^2,\qquad \Delta x = x - 1$$
What the example shows

The tangent is the linear part of the function near the point; the parabola departs from it by the square of the distance to the point of tangency.

Example 3notationfrom the definition

One operation, different letters: $x^3$, $z^3$ and $x^3 + y^3$

The goal

Make sure that $\frac{df}{dx}$, $\frac{df}{dz}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ are one and the same operation.

  1. $f(x) = x^3$, an increment in $x$.

    $$\frac{(x + \Delta x)^3 - x^3}{\Delta x} = 3x^2 + 3x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2 \to 3x^2 = \frac{df}{dx}$$

  2. $f(z) = z^3$: the same function, a different letter.

    $$\frac{(z + \Delta z)^3 - z^3}{\Delta z} \to 3z^2 = \frac{df}{dz}$$

  3. $f(x, y) = x^3 + y^3$: only $x$ gets an increment, and the term $y^3$ cancels.

    $$\frac{(x + \Delta x)^3 + y^3 - x^3 - y^3}{\Delta x} \to 3x^2 = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$$
What the example shows

The letter in the denominator names the variable that gets the increment. The symbol $\partial$ merely reminds you that other variables exist and are frozen.

Example 4from the definition

$f(x) = \frac1x$, $x \ne 0$: a fraction from the definition

The goal

Find the derivative of a fraction via a common denominator and check the sign.

  1. The increment, over a common denominator.

    $$\Delta f = \frac{1}{x + \Delta x} - \frac1x = \frac{-\Delta x}{x(x + \Delta x)}$$

  2. Divide by $\Delta x$ and pass to the limit.

    $$\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = -\frac{1}{x(x + \Delta x)} \to -\frac{1}{x^2}$$

  3. At $x = 2$ the function is decreasing.

    $$f'(2) = -\frac14 < 0$$
What the example shows

A minus sign in the derivative means decrease: the larger $x$, the smaller $\frac1x$. The rate of decrease falls off like $\frac{1}{x^2}$.

Example 5existencefrom the definition

$|x|$: where the derivative exists and where it does not

The goal

Apply the definition where the rules “do not work”, and see the role of one-sided limits.

  1. The point $x = 1$: for small $\Delta x$ the number $1 + \Delta x > 0$.

    $$\frac{|1 + \Delta x| - 1}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta x} = 1\ \Rightarrow\ f'(1) = 1$$

  2. The point $x = 0$: different values on the right and on the left.

    $$\frac{|\Delta x|}{\Delta x} = \begin{cases} 1, & \Delta x > 0\\ -1, & \Delta x < 0 \end{cases}$$

  3. The limits $1$ and $-1$ differ.

    $$f'(0)\ \text{does not exist}$$
What the example shows

The derivative is a local property: the same function can have it at some points and not at others. At a kink there is no single tangent.

Example 6two variablesfrom the definition

Partial derivatives of $f = x^2 y + \sin y$ and the gradient at $(1,\ 0)$

The goal

Find both partial derivatives from the definition and assemble the gradient.

  1. With respect to $x$: $y$ is frozen, $\sin y$ cancels.

    $$\frac{(x + \Delta x)^2 y + \sin y - x^2 y - \sin y}{\Delta x} = (2x + \Delta x)\,y \to 2xy$$

  2. With respect to $y$: the fundamental limit for the sine.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\frac{x^2(y + \Delta y) + \sin(y + \Delta y) - x^2 y - \sin y}{\Delta y}\\ &\qquad= x^2 + \frac{\sin(y + \Delta y) - \sin y}{\Delta y} \to x^2 + \cos y\end{aligned}$$

  3. The gradient and its value at $(1,\ 0)$.

    $$\nabla f = (2xy,\ x^2 + \cos y),\qquad \nabla f(1,\ 0) = (0,\ 2)$$
What the example shows

At the point $(1,\ 0)$ the function is “flat” in $x$ and grows at rate $2$ in $y$: $f$ grows fastest straight up, along the $y$ axis.

Example 7three variablesunit vectors

Nabla for $f(x, y, z) = x^2 y + \sin z$

The goal

Build the gradient of a function of three variables and write it in terms of the unit vectors $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$.

  1. With respect to $x$ ($y$, $z$ frozen).

    $$\frac{\Delta_x f}{\Delta x} = \frac{(x + \Delta x)^2 y - x^2 y}{\Delta x} = (2x + \Delta x)\,y \to 2xy$$

  2. With respect to $y$.

    $$\frac{\Delta_y f}{\Delta y} = \frac{x^2(y + \Delta y) - x^2 y}{\Delta y} = x^2$$

  3. With respect to $z$: the difference of sines and the fundamental limit.

    $$\frac{\sin(z + \Delta z) - \sin z}{\Delta z} \to \cos z$$

  4. Two equivalent ways of writing it.

    $$\nabla f = (2xy,\ x^2,\ \cos z) = 2xy\,\mathbf{i} + x^2\,\mathbf{j} + \cos z\,\mathbf{k}$$
What the example shows

As many variables, as many components of the gradient. Each one comes from the same definition: one variable gets the increment.

Example 8directionfrom the definition

The directional derivative: towards the centre of the bowl

The goal

Find the slope of $f = x^2 + y^2$ at the point $(3,\ 4)$ in the direction of the origin and compare it with $|\nabla f|$.

  1. The direction towards the origin: $\mathbf{v} = (-3,\ -4)$, length $5$.

    $$\mathbf{u} = \Big(-\frac35,\ -\frac45\Big) = (-0.6,\ -0.8)$$

  2. From the definition.

    $$\frac{(3 - 0.6t)^2 + (4 - 0.8t)^2 - 25}{t} = \frac{-10t + t^2}{t} = -10 + t \to -10$$

The direction $-\nabla f$ is the direction of fastest decrease, and the slope in it equals $-|\nabla f|$.

Example 10extremum

The minimum of a function: $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$

The goal

Find the minimum point of $f = x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 4y$ using the gradient.

Example 12two variablesfrom the definitiongeometry

The bowl $f = x^2 + y^2$ at $(2,\ 1)$: the gradient and slices

The goal

At a single point, compute the value, both partial derivatives from the definition, the gradient and its length, and understand what the slices and level curves show.

  1. The value and the level curves. The section of the bowl by the horizontal plane $f = c$ is the circle $x^2 + y^2 = c$ of radius $\sqrt{c}$.

    $$f(2,\ 1) = 4 + 1 = 5,\qquad f(1,\ 0) = 1,\ f(2,\ 0) = 4,\ f(2,\ 2) = 8$$

  2. With respect to $x$ ($y$ frozen).

    $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{(x + \Delta x)^2 + y^2 - x^2 - y^2}{\Delta x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}(2x + \Delta x) = 2x$$

  3. With respect to $y$ ($x$ frozen).

    $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0}\frac{x^2 + (y + \Delta y)^2 - x^2 - y^2}{\Delta y} = \lim_{\Delta y \to 0}(2y + \Delta y) = 2y$$

  4. At the point $(2,\ 1)$.

    $$\nabla f(2,\ 1) = (4,\ 2),\qquad |\nabla f| = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47$$

  5. Slices. Freeze $y = 1$: what remains is the parabola $f(x, 1) = x^2 + 1$ with slope $4$ at $x = 2$, so a step of $1$ to the right raises the tangent by $4$. Freeze $x = 2$: $f(2, y) = 4 + y^2$, slope $2$ at $y = 1$.

If you change $y_0$, the parabola $f(x, y_0) = x^2 + y_0^2$ only shifts up or down, while its slope stays the same: the variable $y$ does not take part in $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x$. The gradient $(4,\ 2)$ is perpendicular to the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 5$ and points outwards, in the direction of steepest ascent (Figures 3 and 4).

No. 1★☆☆☆☆

Find the derivative from the definition.

$$f(x) = 5$$
Solution · 4 steps

  1. Write the definition: the limit of the ratio of the increment of the function to the increment of the argument.

    $$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}$$

  2. Find the increment of the function $\Delta f = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)$: substitute $x + \Delta x$ for $x$ and simplify.

    $$\Delta f = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) = 5 - 5 = 0$$

  3. Divide by $\Delta x$ (in the definition of the limit $\Delta x \ne 0$, so this is legitimate) and cancel.

    $$\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{0}{\Delta x} = 0$$

  4. Pass to the limit $\Delta x \to 0$: what remains is continuous in $\Delta x$, so we substitute $\Delta x = 0$.

    $$f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} 0 = 0$$

Find the gradient from the definition.

  1. Write the definition: the limit of the ratio of the increment of the function to the increment of the argument.

The directional derivative, the differential, descent

No. 42★★★★☆

Find the directional derivative from the definition and check it through the gradient.

$$f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2,\quad (1,\ 1),\quad \mathbf{u} = \big(\tfrac35,\ \tfrac45\big)$$
Solution · 4 steps

  1. The definition of the directional derivative: move from the point by $t$ along the unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ and divide the increment by $t$.

    $$D_{\mathbf{u}} f(1,\ 1) = \lim_{t \to 0}\frac{f\big(1 + \frac35 t,\ 1 + \frac45 t\big) - f(1,\ 1)}{t}$$

  2. Compute the increment of the function along the direction and divide by $t$.

    $$\frac{\big(1 + \frac35 t\big)^2 + \big(1 + \frac45 t\big)^2 - 2}{t} = \frac{\frac{14}{5}t + t^2}{t} = \frac{14}{5} + t$$

  3. Pass to the limit $t \to 0$.

    $$D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \frac{14}{5}$$

  4. Check through the gradient: $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}$ — the same answer.

    $$\nabla f(1,\ 1) = (2,\ 2),\qquad (2,\ 2)\cdot\big(\tfrac35,\ \tfrac45\big) = \frac65 + \frac85 = \frac{14}{5}$$
Answer
$$D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \frac{14}{5}$$

Test yourself

1. What does $\Delta x$ mean?
$\Delta x = x_{\text{new}} - x_{\text{old}}$ is a single number, the increment.
2. What is the limit $\lim\limits_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{(x + \Delta x)^2 - x^2}{\Delta x}$?
$\frac{2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2}{\Delta x} = 2x + \Delta x \to 2x$.
3. $f(x, y) = x^2 y + y^3$. What is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$?
$y$ is frozen, and $y^3$ does not depend on $x$, so it gives zero.
4. How many components does the gradient of $f(x_1, \ldots, x_{100})$ have?
As many variables, as many partial derivatives, as many components.
5. In which direction does a function grow fastest?
$D_{\mathbf{u}} f = |\nabla f|\cos\varphi$ is maximal at $\varphi = 0$.
6. $f = x^2 + y^2$, a descent step with $\eta = 1$. What happens?
The coordinates are multiplied by $1 - 2\eta = -1$: endless oscillation.
7. How does $df$ differ from $\nabla f$?
$df = \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{x}$ is the dot product of the gradient and the step.

Summary

ConceptFormula from the definition
Increment$\Delta x = x_{\text{new}} - x_{\text{old}},\ \ \Delta y = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)$
Slope of a line / secant$k = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$
Derivative$f'(x) = \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \lim\limits_{\Delta x \to 0}\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$
Differential$dy = f'(x)\,dx$
Partial derivative$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = \lim\limits_{\Delta x_i \to 0}\dfrac{\Delta_{x_i} f}{\Delta x_i}$
Gradient$\nabla f = \Big(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\ \ldots,\ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\Big)$
Directional derivative$D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \lim\limits_{t \to 0}\dfrac{f(\mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{x})}{t} = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}$ ($|\mathbf{u}| = 1$, $f$ differentiable)
Total differential$df = \sum\limits_i \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i = \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{x}$
Gradient descent$\mathbf{p} \leftarrow \mathbf{p} - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{p})$

One idea ties the whole table together: take a small step, look at the ratio of the changes and pass to the limit. The derivative is for one variable, partial derivatives are with respect to one variable out of many, the gradient is all the partial derivatives together, and gradient descent uses it to find minima. The next step is the chain rule: the derivative of a composite function and the foundation of backpropagation.
